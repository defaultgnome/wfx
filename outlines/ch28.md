## 1. Purpose

Part VIII looks back. Chapter 3 said every choice is a lease with a hidden exit fee. This chapter presents the bills: five migrations (renderer, scene format, storage, scheduler, language runtime), told as short case files, each of which nearly went wrong, and what they have in common.

Two of the five were already told in full: the scene format in Ch. 5 and the renderer in Ch. 8. Here each gets one short paragraph as a callback. The new material is the **playbook** and the three cases not told elsewhere: storage, scheduler, runtime.

The chapter's thesis: *a migration is not a project with an end date. It is a period of running two systems at once, and the hard part is the overlap.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"The old system never goes away on the day you said it would."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**A way of migrating**

- Why migrations fail socially and operationally more often than technically: the new thing works, but nobody can leave the old thing yet.
- The idea of **the overlap period**: how long it lasts, what it costs, and why it always lasts longer than planned.
- Techniques that kept us alive: run old and new side by side, compare outputs on real shots (the pattern is introduced in Ch. 5 and Ch. 8; not re-taught here), migrate by shot or by show instead of by system, keep a way back until a defined point.
- The **parity trap**: promising the new system will match the old one exactly, when the old one's quirks were the real specification.
- How to pick a migration window in a facility with no quiet season (link to Ch. 25 on change freezes).
- A lightweight **migration checklist**, introduced at the end as the chapter's takeaway (a closing callout, not a template to fill in mid-story).

**The five case files** (listed in the order of the book's chapters; the real chronology is stated once, below)

- Renderer (Ch. 8): callback paragraph
- Scene format (Ch. 5): callback paragraph
- Storage (Ch. 7): new case
- Scheduler (Ch. 19): new case
- A scripting-language runtime (Ch. 24): new case

**The real chronology (stated once, here)**

The scene-format migration started first. The renderer migration followed, partly overlapping it. Then storage, then the scheduler, then the runtime. Only the last three were close to sequential. In the middle of it all, the farm kept rendering a million tasks a night.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Technical depth on each system; those chapters already have it. Here we tell what went wrong in *moving*.
- Vendor comparisons.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **the day the migration was declared done.** A dashboard says 100%. Then an artist on a show that wrapped a year earlier reopens a shot for a re-delivery, and it does not look the way it did. Light, rueful. The line: the migration was finished; the shot was not.

This is the cousin of Ch. 6's mystery (the old shot that re-rendered with a blue shadow, which turned out to be a plugin update). Different cause here: the renderer's behavior had changed in a corner nobody tested. Say so in one sentence, and note that Ch. 21's cloud-vs-local variant remains the unresolved cousin.

*(illustrative)* The scene: a soft-edged shadow under the giant's foot, rendered one way in the archive and slightly harder in the new renderer, a fix of three hours for one shot and four days to find out how many others.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

Migrations as a straight line: build the new thing, switch, switch off the old. A plan with a tidy Gantt chart and a "decommission" milestone. One dry sentence on how often the decommission milestone moved (four times, and then it became a "target").

### The chronology, once (≈100 words)

A short passage that states the real order (see section 2) so the case files do not each have to explain it, and notes that the overlaps are why the facility was running up to three "old" things at once in a bad quarter.

### Case file 1: The renderer (≈150 words, one paragraph)

Callback only. What moved is in Ch. 8, including the look-match program and artist retraining. The one new angle: the **overlap period**. Shows already in flight were allowed to finish on the old renderer, and that rule is why the overlap ran far longer than planned: it was tied to the slowest show, not to the calendar.

*(illustrative)* Planned overlap: four months. Actual: nine. During the overlap, a mid-show renderer-upgrade freeze (Ch. 25) deferred the cutover for one show by a month.

### Case file 2: The scene format (≈150 words, one paragraph)

Callback only. What moved is in Ch. 5, including the fidelity test suite and the rule that nothing was retired until the last user was found. The one new angle: the **long tail**. The last ten percent of readers were one-off scripts, and finding them dominated the overlap. Point to Ch. 5 for how.

*(illustrative)* Planned overlap: six months. Actual: fourteen.

### Case file 3: Storage (≈600 words)

(Setup in Ch. 7's honest footnote.) What nearly went wrong: moving petabytes while artists kept writing to them. Data gravity in miniature: the copy never finishes if the source keeps changing. Also paths baked into old scenes and caches (link to Ch. 6 on provenance). The save: moving by project, redirecting rather than rewriting paths, and verifying by checksum before anything was deleted.

*(illustrative)* The near miss: a project folder marked "copied and verified" had a cache directory that was still being written by a forgotten overnight job on the farm. The old copy was one click from deletion. A last pre-delete check of "last modified in the past week" stopped it; the giant's final comp had a week left.

### Case file 4: The scheduler (≈600 words)

(Setup in Ch. 19: the scheduler described there is the *old* one.) What nearly went wrong: the old scheduler's fairness rules lived unwritten in code, and the politics of the queue (who gets priority) resurfaced as "bugs" in the new one. Writing the policy down, the thing Ch. 19 shows in its finished form, is what the replacement forced. The save: writing the rules down first, then running both queues in shadow mode and comparing which tasks each would have started.

*(illustrative)* The surprise: a rule nobody remembered letting the shortest tasks jump the line at night "because artists were waiting on them". The new queue did not have it; the first Monday after a trial, three supervisors asked why their thumbnails were slow.

### Case file 5: The language runtime (≈500 words)

The least glamorous and most widespread: moving the scripting runtime that every tool and DCC plugin depended on (the maintenance tax foreshadowed in Ch. 24). What nearly went wrong: thousands of small scripts, some with no owner (the Ch. 3 bolt-on trap), a few of them load-bearing. The save: an inventory by actually running things and logging what they imported, not by asking around.

*(illustrative)* The inventory found about 3,000 scripts, some 400 with no owner, and one that nobody believed existed, which resized thumbnails on Fridays.

> **Visual:** one timeline strip per case file: planned overlap vs. actual overlap (the illustrative figures above).

### What the five have in common (≈450 words)

The reframe. Pull the pattern across the cases without turning it into a list in the prose:

- The old system is a specification nobody wrote down.
- Overlap costs more than either system alone.
- The last 10% is the long tail of one-off users.
- Reversibility is worth paying for until a deliberate, announced point of no return.

Then the **migration checklist** as a short closing callout (five or six lines: inventory the users, define parity, shadow-run, migrate in slices, set the point of no return, then turn it off and mean it). Decision: it is a callout, not woven into the narrative.

### Closing: What stuck (≈250 words)

Return to the opening shot. The answer to "is it done?" is now a question: *done for whom?* What stuck: *you don't finish a migration, you stop being afraid of the old system.* Bridge to Ch. 29: migrations are the dramatic version of change; the daily version is technical debt, and it has no completion date at all.

## 6. Details to get right

- Keep each new case file short and shaped the same way: what moved, what almost broke, what saved it.
- Cases 1 and 2 stay one paragraph each; do not re-tell Ch. 5 or Ch. 8.
- Chronology is stated once (section 3); case files do not restate it.
- All shows, vendors and people are anonymous; numbers are illustrative orders of magnitude.
- Don't claim any migration went perfectly. "Nearly went wrong" means it also nearly succeeded: say what held.
- Don't re-teach the system. Point back to the owning chapter.
