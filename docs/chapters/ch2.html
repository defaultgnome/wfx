    The Scale We Actually Deal With

    Scale isn't one number. It's four, on different axes, and each breaks a different part of the system. The first thing to break is rarely the one you'd guess.

    Chapter 2Continues from Chapter 112 min readDraft

    Let's multiply the giant. It won't take long, and it ruins the next hour.

    Our giant's shot is sixty frames long: two and a half seconds at 24 frames a second. Each frame is rendered as roughly thirty separate layers (lighting passes, depth, masks, the usual cast), so one full render of the shot is about 1,800 images. The shot went through about forty versions before the director stopped finding things to love and change. Every version re-renders everything, so the shot's life adds up to around 72,000 images. For two and a half seconds of film.

    Now widen the lens. A show has somewhere between 800 and 2,000 shots, most of them cheaper than the giant. But pretend, for the arithmetic, that all 2,000 were giants: that's 144 million images for one show. Say only a tenth survive as complete renders, 14 million files, and we have about eight shows in flight at once. That's some 115 million files, and at tens of megabytes each, typical for high-resolution floating-point images, a few petabytes of pictures alone. We haven't mentioned a single simulation cache. And the studio makes about ten thousand shots a year, which is where the title of this book comes from; if every one of them were a giant, the studio would be minting 720 million images a year, for the renders alone.

    When our storage team first saw projections like these, they wanted to talk about terabytes. I wanted to talk about the other number. A hundred and fifteen million files isn't a size problem. It's a counting problem, and counting turns out to be an entirely different discipline.

    A word on the numbers

    As in Chapter 1, the giant is a heavy composite, and the figures here are orders of magnitude, not audited totals. An average shot costs a small fraction of what he does. The point of the exercise is the shape of the growth, not the third significant digit.

    The disks fantasy

    For a good while, I believed scale was a procurement problem. Things get big, so you buy bigger things. There is a spreadsheet, a purchase order, a loading dock, and a satisfying moment when someone wheels in a very large box that blinks.

    This approach works beautifully for exactly one of the ways a system can fail, and it happens to be the least interesting one. Running out of space is loud, obvious, and fixed with money. Most of what actually goes wrong at scale is quiet, strange, and not fixed with money at all. To tell the difference, it helps to stop saying “scale” as though it were a single dial.

    Four kinds of big

    In a pipeline, I find it useful to separate scale into four axes. Each has its own numbers, and each fails in its own way.

    Bytes

    Petabytes per show. Chapter 1's table showed where the bulk lives: simulation caches and rendered layers, both made by machines. The dangerous failure here isn't running out, which is loud and gets fixed. It's the quiet habit of keeping everything “just in case,” until the cost of the case becomes one of the largest lines in the budget. Ch. 22

    Objects

    Hundreds of millions of files over a show's life. Directories holding a hundred thousand entries. Millions of versions, and millions more rows in the databases that describe them. Reading a file is cheap if you know where it is. Finding out what's there is the expensive part: listing, checking, opening. A tool that politely “just looks in the folder” can bring a file system to its knees, because ten thousand polite tools did it at once. Ch. 7

    Compute

    Here is the farm, stated once so the rest of the book can lean on it: about 5,000 nodes (machines), each with 64 cores, so about 320,000 cores. A standard node carries 512 GB of memory, and a smaller high-memory pool carries 1 TB; whenever a later chapter mentions a truly large per-task footprint, that is where it runs. Across the facility that adds up to a few hundred million core-hours a year, and one big show alone can use tens of millions.

    On a busy night the farm works through about a million tasks, the individual units of work such as one frame of one layer. Artists don't submit them one at a time. They submit jobs, requests of about 240 tasks each, so that million tasks is roughly 4,000 jobs. (Chapter 19 untangles the two words; until then, I'll say tasks.) Frames take hours each, and the farm is a shared utility with a permanent queue. The failure isn't “too slow.” It's “too contended.” Two shows in crunch want the same thousand machines on the same night, and the interesting engineering becomes politics conducted through a queue-based scheduler. Ch. 19

    Two more ideas belong here. The first is the long tail: most shots are ordinary and a few are monsters, a crowd of thousands or a simulation that fills a rack of memory, and it's the monsters, the 99th percentile, that set your infrastructure limits. The second is the rate of change. It isn't only how much data exists but how fast it is created, rewritten and read, and a quiet archive is a very different animal from a folder that thirty people touch every minute.

    People

    About 500 artists across two sites (roughly 300 at one and 200 at the other), a few dozen pipeline engineers, about a thousand staff in all, and one review room. This is the axis nobody puts on a capacity chart, and it's the one that eventually binds. You cannot watch more shots in dailies than the day has hours. At some point your farm can produce more candidate shots than the humans can judge, and the bottleneck quietly migrates from machines to eyeballs, where no amount of hardware helps.

    Peaks, not averages

    Every number above is an average, and averages are the least useful thing you can know about a production. A show doesn't produce work evenly. It ramps up slowly, sits through a long plateau where things are busy but manageable, and then, in the last stretch before delivery, and most savagely in the final six weeks, load climbs like a wall, a multiple of a normal week.

    This shape poses an awkward question. If you provision the farm for the peak, it sits half-idle for most of the show, which is expensive. If you provision for the average, it is a lovely, efficient machine for most of a show and a catastrophe for the last six weeks, which is exactly when nobody can afford one. There isn't a free answer, and we'll come back to the least bad ones, including renting capacity only when you need it. Ch. 21

    There's a second rhythm on top of the first. Artists work in broadly the same hours, so load also surges every day, when a few hundred people hit Publish before the evening review. And shows overlap: while one is in its crunch, the next has begun to ramp. A system sized for “normal” rarely meets normal.

    What breaks first

    So much for the theory. Here is the list I wish someone had handed me in my first year: five things that tend to break as a pipeline grows, roughly in the order they appear. I've learned to expect most of them in some form, and I still keep getting surprised.

    1. 1

      A very long directory listing

      Shows up early, when a folder passes ~100,000 entries

      One of our texture folders grew to about 200,000 entries, each one a small file. A tool that politely “just checked the folder” every time it launched began asking the file system “what's in here?” and waiting minutes for the answer. It wasn't broken; it was thinking. Because artists and render nodes share the same file servers, everyone's tools slowed together, including the ones that never went near that folder, and the floor lost most of an afternoon.

      My first move was to order faster disks. The disks were fine. The data was tiny; the pain was entirely in the number of files, and the fix was a directory layout rule and a short lecture to myself.

      Why it surprised us
      Nothing was big. The pain was entirely in the bookkeeping about the data.
      The dull fix
      Spread files across directories by design, and teach tools to look things up instead of listing them. Ch. 7
    2. 2

      The hot file

      Shows up when the farm gets big

      A single scene file or texture that three thousand render tasks open at the same moment. Each task needs only a few megabytes. Together they ask one server for the same bytes in the same second, and the server answers them, correctly, in a line. The farm sits there waiting for storage, with CPU graphs that look like a calm sea. Everything is healthy and nothing is happening.

      Why it surprised us
      Every individual request was reasonable. The problem lived entirely in their sum.
      The dull fix
      Caching close to the machines, and never trusting one copy to serve a crowd. Ch. 7
    3. 3

      The license

      Shows up when the farm outgrows its contracts

      Some commercial tools need a license for each running process. A farm can have ten thousand idle cores and eight hundred licenses, and then the limit isn't hardware or code but a number in a contract. Tasks sit “ready,” machines sit empty, and the only thing missing is permission. It's a non-technical limit that behaves exactly like a technical one.

      Why it surprised us
      You can't profile a contract.
      The dull fix
      Treat licenses as a schedulable resource, like memory, and make their consumption visible. Ch. 19
    4. 4

      The scheduler's own database

      Shows up when the queue holds a million tasks

      The system that tracks a million tasks a night has to be faster than the work it tracks. Our queue-based scheduler spent one stretch of its life as the slowest thing on the entire farm, mostly occupied with writing down that it was busy. A scheduler that can't keep up with its own bookkeeping makes the whole farm look broken while every machine in it is fine.

      Why it surprised us
      We'd been optimizing the work. The thing managing the work was the bottleneck.
      The dull fix
      Group tasks into jobs, summarize rather than record, and treat the control plane as a product with its own capacity. Ch. 19
    5. 5

      The review room

      Shows up when everything else finally works

      At some point the farm was healthy, the storage was calm, and shots arrived faster than anyone could look at them. The queue of “ready for review” kept growing. The throughput of decisions had become the cap on the whole show. The most valuable optimization that month was not a faster renderer but a better way to decide what the director should look at first.

      Why it surprised us
      It meant every earlier optimization had a ceiling, and the ceiling wasn't a machine.
      The dull fix
      Better triage, better tools for the reviewers, and a plain acknowledgement that people have limits. Ch. 23

    My own record at guessing the next bottleneck is, I'm sorry to say, about one in three, and the folder story above is how I learned to say so out loud. I've stopped trying to predict. I try to measure early, so that the next surprise arrives as a graph rather than as a phone call.

    One more honest footnote on the whole exercise. Every item on this list got fixed, and none of the fixes were permanent. A directory rule holds until someone invents a new way to make a folder large, and a cache holds until the farm doubles. Scale doesn't get solved so much as rescheduled, and the useful question is always which count will hurt next year.

    Thinking about scale

    If there's a skill hidden in all this, it's less about knowing the answers than about asking the right small questions early. I've settled on three habits, all cheap. They sit on a way of thinking in orders of magnitude, on a log scale, as in Figure 2.1, where a thousand and a million are a few steps apart rather than a chasm.

    Budget per frame, per file, per second. A big number is impossible to reason about, and the same number divided into small pieces becomes something you can argue over. “A hundred million files” is a mood. “One file every ten milliseconds, for a week” is a design constraint.

    Count the objects before you weigh them. Ask “how many?” before “how big?” Most of the nasty surprises in this chapter came from counts, not sizes.

    Design for ten times; be suspicious of a hundred. A design that handles ten times today's load usually has comfortable headroom. A design that has to handle a hundred times is usually a different design, and it's much cheaper to learn that on a whiteboard.

    Before building anything, ask
    • How many of these will there be, and how many at the same moment?
    • What happens to the hundred-thousandth one?
    • Who, or what, has to wait for it?
    • What does “the last six weeks” look like for this?

    What stuck

    I think about the giant, multiplied by a thousand, more than is probably healthy. He's patient about it. What strikes me most is how unglamorous the work turns out to be. Most of the engineering in this book is not cleverness; it is counting things, noticing which count is about to hurt, and moving it out of the way before it does.

    Takeaway

    Bytes are rarely what hurt you first. Count the things.

    It also changed how I read a plan. When someone shows me a design now, I skip to the numbers slide and ask how many of each thing there will be on the worst night of the last month of the biggest show. Nobody has ever been offended by the question. A few have gone quiet and then gone off to do some arithmetic, which is the right reaction, and the one I had myself in front of a folder with 200,000 entries.

    Which leaves a question. If a problem is this big and this strange, who builds the tools to deal with it, and what do you simply buy? That's Chapter 3, and it's where I get to confess some of my worst decisions.
