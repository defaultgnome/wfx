# Chapter 22: Cost and Energy Accounting

**Part VI: The Farm and the Cloud**
**Target length:** ~3,200 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 21 (the invoice) and Chapter 19 (the queue as the place of scarcity); retries and waste also pick up Chapter 20

---

## 1. Purpose

Part VI closes here. The farm can start a million tasks a night (Ch. 19), survives its own failures (Ch. 20), and can rent extra capacity (Ch. 21). Chapter 22 asks the uncomfortable follow-up: *who knows what any of it costs?*

The chapter's thesis: *Compute feels free to the person who submits it. Visibility, not restriction, is what changes behavior, and the metric you choose will be optimized, sometimes against your intent.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"You can't ask people to spend wisely until they can see what they spend."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**What there is to measure**

- **Core-hours** (and GPU-hours) as the basic unit, defined in a clause, and why even that is slippery: a core-hour on an old machine is not a core-hour on a new one.
- Beyond time: memory occupied, storage held, data moved, licenses consumed, and the electricity behind all of it. For scale (illustrative): a few hundred million core-hours a year for the facility, tens of millions for one big show.
- **Cost per frame** and **cost per shot**: the natural-sounding metrics and the traps in them. Appendix C keeps them with a counter-metric, and this chapter says why.
- **Waste:** work that finished and was thrown away (re-renders, canceled tasks, killed preemptions from Ch. 19, retries from Ch. 20), versus work that was legitimately iterated.
- Idle capacity: the cost of machines that are on and doing nothing.

**Making it visible**

- Tagging every task with *who and what it was for*: user, show, department, purpose (test, review, final).
- Attribution is a data-plumbing problem first: if tasks cannot be traced to a shot, nothing downstream works (link to Ch. 6 provenance).
- **Showback versus chargeback:** showing people their usage versus billing it. Why we began with the gentler one and what changed when money entered the picture.
- Dashboards for three audiences: the artist ("what did my last render cost?"), the supervisor ("where is the show's budget going?") and the facility ("what is the farm doing?").

**Behavior, and the metrics that backfire**

- The first time a cost number appeared next to a render button and what artists did with it (some thought harder; some stopped test-rendering, which was the opposite of the intent).
- Metrics that get gamed (the rule that a measure turned into a target stops measuring; plain description first, name second): cost per frame improves by rendering fewer, easier frames; utilization improves by running useless tasks; "waste" shrinks if nobody labels anything as waste.
- Why artistic iteration must be protected: the cheapest shot is the one nobody looked at.
- Nudges that worked better than limits: cheaper low-resolution defaults, previews before finals (Ch. 11), and visible estimates *before* a submission.

**Energy**

- The facility's compute has a power bill and a footprint. This is the chapter's single use of the "very expensive space heater" image (Chapter 20 owns it; echo it here once, in a clause, and nowhere else).
- What can be measured (power per machine, per rack, per task) and what can only be estimated; how to be honest about the difference.
- Levers: efficient hardware, tuning the renderer's sampling (Ch. 10), turning idle machines off, shifting flexible work to cooler or cleaner hours, and choosing where cloud work runs (Ch. 21).
- The honest tension: more time-efficient rendering has not meant less energy used, because artists reinvest savings in richer shots.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A minimal set of tags that makes usage attributable.
- A short list of metrics worth tracking, plus the ones to approach with suspicion (feeds Appendix C).
- The habit of publishing a number with a sentence about how it can mislead.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Contract pricing, budgets and any real financial figures.
- Cloud contract mechanics and discounting (Ch. 21 covered the shape).
- Carbon accounting standards and certification detail; mention in general terms only.
- Scheduler policy design (Ch. 19).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **a number nobody had seen before.** The first report that attributed farm usage to individual shots. One test render, left to repeat all weekend by a forgotten script, accounted for a startling share of a night's compute. No one was at fault; no one had been able to know.

Then the line: the farm had been a shared pool for years, and a shared pool is where nobody can see themselves.

> **Illustrative detail:** a single test render, set to repeat by a forgotten script, took about one-twelfth of a Saturday night's compute across about 3,000 of its 5,000 nodes. It had been running, unseen, for most of a month.

### The comfortable belief (≈200 words)

Cost control is a finance problem: get a number, set a budget, enforce it. Light humor about the first budget spreadsheet and its confident assumption that every task knew whom it belonged to.

### You can't charge what you can't attribute (≈600 words)

The plumbing section, told as a problem.

- Why it was hard to say whose a task was: scripts that submit on others' behalf, shared accounts, tasks that serve several shots, and untagged legacy tools.
- The minimum viable tagging: user, show, shot, department, purpose.
- Where the tags come from: made automatic by the submission tools, not entered by hand, because anything optional gets skipped (the lesson Chapter 23 will explore next as a matter of tool design; a forward pointer, not a callback).
- The unattributed remainder: how we shrank it and learned to publish it rather than hide it.

> **Visual:** a flow diagram from total farm hours to show, department and purpose, with a visible "unattributed" slice.

### Measuring a core-hour (≈550 words)

- Machines differ: normalizing to a reference unit, and the honest imprecision.
- What cost per frame hides: resolution, complexity, retries, the cost of the preceding failed attempts.
- Cost of waste versus cost of iteration: how we separated "thrown away because we changed our minds" from "thrown away because something broke".
- Idle machines and the case for turning them off.

Include a table: metric, what it tells you, how it misleads. Seed the contents of Appendix C.

### Visibility changes behavior (≈750 words)

The heart of the chapter.

- **Showback first.** The first dashboards, and how supervisors used them differently from artists.
- **The estimate before you submit.** A rough cost shown next to the submit action. In this telling (illustrative) the most effective change we made, and the cheapest.
- **The backfire (illustrative).** Artists who began skipping test renders because someone had put a number on them. Then the reframe: tests are the cheap insurance against expensive finals, so we measured *wasted finals* and left tests alone.
- **Metrics that got gamed:** two or three plausible gaming stories, told with the author as the one who set the metric.
- **Chargeback:** what happens when budgets are real; how departments started negotiating about the queue (a loop back to Ch. 19's politics).

Keep the tone fair: people respond rationally to what is measured.

### Energy: the room that gets warm (≈550 words)

- Start with the thing everyone can feel: the machine room's temperature and the electricity line. One clause only: the farm, as Ch. 20 put it, is a very expensive space heater, and the machine room was the proof.
- What we can measure directly and what we can only estimate, and why we say which is which.
- Practical levers: switching off idle nodes, scheduling flexible work for off-peak, tuning sampling so frames stop at "good enough" (Ch. 10), efficient hardware refreshes, and where cloud work runs (Ch. 21).
- The rebound effect, honestly: efficiency gains were spent on richer images, not saved. That is not a failure; it is what creative work does.

> **Illustrative detail:** the facility measures power per rack, not per task; per-task energy is an estimate, and every dashboard says so in its footnote. Turning off idle nodes overnight saved a visible slice of the bill and cost one morning of complaints about cold starts.

### The honest footnote (≈200 words)

What isn't solved: attribution for shared assets and caches; a single trustworthy "cost of a shot"; comparing energy across on-premises and cloud honestly; and the risk that a good dashboard becomes a performance review nobody asked for.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the weekend test render: now it shows up in Monday's report before anyone's coffee, and the person who owns it gets a friendly message instead of a lecture.
- "What I'd tell my past self" (the Part VI to VII closing style continues here (Ch. 20 and 21 already use it), a deliberate split from the "What stuck" closings of Chapters 1 to 19): *the goal is not a smaller number; it is a number someone can act on. And publish the unattributed slice on day one.*
- **Bridge to Part VII:** the farm is a system of machines and policies, but the people who use it are what matters. Chapter 23 begins with the tools artists actually use, and with the lesson that anything optional gets skipped.
