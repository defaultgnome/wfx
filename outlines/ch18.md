**Part V: Characters and Performance**

## 1. Purpose

Part V closes with the object that sits between performance and picture: the **rig**, the control structure that lets an animator (or a capture stream) pose and deform a character. Rigs look like art assets and behave like programs. They have inputs, logic, dependencies, bugs, performance problems, and releases.

The chapter's thesis: *a rig is software that happens to be built by people who don't call themselves engineers, and treating it that way, with versions, tests and profiling, is what makes a character survive a year of production.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"A rig is a program whose users are animators and whose bugs show up on a monster's face."*

It also closes Part V with a one-line wrap (the performance is captured, carried live, and now built to last) and hands off to Part VI: once characters are posed, they must be rendered by the farm.

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Mental models**

- What a rig is, in a clause for developers: a graph of controls and deformers that turns a small number of animator inputs into a large amount of moving geometry. *Rig* and *skinning* (binding a surface to a skeleton) were first used in Ch. 13, 14 and 16; here is the deep treatment. Define *control*, *deformer* and *evaluation* in a clause each.
- Rigs are **dataflow programs**: nodes, connections, and an evaluation order. This is familiar territory to engineers, and the key to testing and profiling them.
- **Rigs change while in use.** A character is rigged early, animated in hundreds of shots, and fixed continually. A fix to the rig must not change work already approved.
- **Performance is a feature.** An animator working at five frames per second instead of twenty-four animates differently and worse. Rig speed shapes the quality of the performance.
- **Versioning rigs** is harder than versioning code: the "source" is a binary file in a creative tool, and the rig is embedded in animation data that references it.
- **Rigs have contracts:** control names, ranges, and behaviors that animation, capture and simulation depend on. Break the contract and shots silently change.

**What this world looks like**

- A hero character may have thousands of controls; a crowd character far fewer. Rig complexity is budgeted like anything else.
- Where rigs live in the pipeline: built by a character department, consumed by animation, capture retargeting (Ch. 16), live stage (Ch. 17), simulation (Ch. 14), and finally the renderer.
- The typical lifecycle: build, test by animating, release, hotfix, freeze, reuse on the next show.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- How to apply ordinary engineering practices to rigs: **a test suite of poses**, a regression comparison, a profiler, and a release process.
- A practical **rig contract** checklist: names, ranges, units, defaults, and what is allowed to change in a patch release.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- How to build a good rig artistically (shape quality, topology, anatomy; see Ch. 14 for the biology).
- Scripting interfaces or code listings of any kind.
- Retargeting and capture mechanics (Ch. 16).
- Detailed DCC plugin maintenance (Ch. 24).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **a character that changed overnight.** A rigger fixes a shoulder that pinched in a few shots. Next morning, a hundred approved shots have a character whose arm sits two centimeters differently, and an animator is asking, politely, why the creature seems to be shrugging at everything. The fix was correct. The change was unannounced. Nobody had called it a release.

> **The story (illustrative composite):** the shoulder fix touched 112 approved shots and was noticed after 26 hours, when an animator asked why the creature seemed to be shrugging. The fix was correct; the change was unannounced.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"A rig is an asset like a chair or a tree: build it, publish it, done." Light humor on the shape of a chair that gets a software update every Tuesday.

### Why a rig is a program (≈450 words)

Walk the reader through the dataflow idea in plain language: inputs, a graph of operations, outputs. Draw the parallel to a spreadsheet or a build system, since both are dependency graphs evaluated in order.

- Where rigs resemble code: dependencies, side effects, hidden global state, and the "works on my machine" version.
- Where they differ: made visually, saved as binary, with no compiler to complain.
- The cultural gap: riggers think in shape and motion; engineers think in contracts. Both are right; the chapter lives between them.

> **Visual:** a tiny rig graph (three controls, two deformers, one mesh) next to the equivalent dependency graph of a build.

### Versioning and contracts (≈550 words)

The first engineering practice.

- Number rig releases in three tiers, borrowed from libraries: major (changes the look), minor (new controls), patch (fixes that must not change approved shots). One plain-language table, no more.
- **The contract:** control names and ranges, and how animation data is stored against them. Renaming a control is a breaking change; say so, loudly.
- **Pinning:** shots should be able to stay on the rig version they were approved with, and move forward by choice. (Back-reference dependency tracking in Ch. 6.)
- What to do when a fix *should* change old shots: opt-in migration with a visible diff.
- Honest cost: pinning keeps multiple rig versions alive, which means more storage and more support.

### Testing a rig (≈500 words)

Make it concrete: what does a test look like for something with no code?

- **Pose libraries:** a standard set of extreme poses and a range-of-motion test, run automatically on every release, compared against the previous version.
- **Regression by picture and by number:** compare vertex positions and also look at rendered turntables, because some failures are visible only to humans.
- **Contract checks:** do all expected controls exist, with the same names and ranges?
- **Stress tests:** extreme values, odd combinations, and the pose the animator will inevitably find that you never imagined.
- What not to automate: judging whether a face looks right. Tests catch changes; people judge quality.

> **Visual:** side-by-side of a pose test, old versus new, with the difference highlighted in color.

### Profiling a rig (≈450 words)

The second engineering practice, and the link to Chapter 17.

- Measuring where the time goes: the few expensive nodes, the hidden evaluations, the deformer that is quietly doing a lot more than its name suggests.
- Budgets: playback speed targets for animators, tighter ones for live stage use, looser for final render-time evaluation. One rig, three speed requirements.
- Why a rig that is fine alone can crawl in a scene of twenty characters, and the useful trick of **lighter versions of the same rig** for distant or background use (the same principle as level of detail for crowds, Ch. 13, which does not itself cover rigs).
- The trade-off between rig quality and speed, stated plainly: someone has to decide which shoulder is worth a frame per second.
- Evaluation on the farm: the same rig, evaluated for every frame of every shot. One clause, as the handoff to Part VI; Ch. 19 and 20 do not cover rig cost.

### Release process and the human side (≈400 words)

- A modest release pipeline: build, auto-test, review by a rigger and an animator, publish, announce, with a changelog written for artists in plain language.
- **Hotfix etiquette** during crunch: how to ship a rig fix without disturbing approved shots. (The same etiquette, applied to crunch generally, appears in Ch. 25.)
- Ownership: who is on call for a rig, and how a character acquires a maintainer for the length of a show.
- Reuse across shows, and why a rig that was good for one film can be a liability on the next.

### Closing (≈250 words)

- One-line callback to the giant: its rig has the same shoulder, and the same release process now protects its forty versions.
- Return to the shrugging creature. Show the same fix now (112 shots pinned and untouched): a patch release with a diff, pinned shots untouched, a note in the artists' channel.
- "What stuck" line: *the most valuable thing we did for rigs was call them software.*
- **Bridge to Part VI:** the character is now built, performed and posed. Every frame of it must be rendered, and there are a lot of frames. Chapter 19 opens the farm: how a million tasks a night find a machine to run on.

## 5. Humor and tone opportunities

- A chair that gets a software update every Tuesday.
- "The fix was correct, which is not the same as safe."
- A rig with a thousand controls and one hidden node that does most of the work.
- Engineers explaining major, minor and patch numbering to a roomful of people who have never wanted to know it, and then agreeing it was a good idea.

Keep the target the author's own learning curve. Be respectful of riggers: the craft is real, and the practices are borrowed to support it, not to replace it.

## 6. Details to get right

- Do not imply riggers are unskilled at engineering; many are strong programmers. The point is practice, not talent.
- Keep any speed numbers illustrative and labeled so.
- Stay tool-neutral: describe rigs generally, not any one product's rig system.
- No confidential show or character details; the opening story is a composite.
- Avoid code. Describe tests and contracts in words.
