# Chapter 17: Real-Time on Set

**Part V: Characters and Performance**
**Target length:** ~3,200 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 16 (the same performance data, now driven live instead of processed later)

---

## 1. Purpose

Every chapter so far has assumed time: hours per frame, nights on the farm, weeks of review. Chapter 17 removes it. On a virtual production stage, the image must be right **now**, thirty or sixty times a second, with a director, a camera operator and a dozen actors waiting.

The chapter's thesis: *real-time work does not run on a smaller version of the offline pipeline. It runs on a different contract: a latency budget, spent carefully, with every stage allowed a few milliseconds.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"Latency is a budget, and everyone is a spender."*

It is also where two cultures meet: a film pipeline built for correctness over days, and game-engine technology built for plausibility in milliseconds. The chapter is about the seam.

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Mental models**

- What **virtual production** is, in plain terms: shooting actors in front of large screens or with live previews of digital content, so decisions are made on the day rather than months later. Define *in-camera visual effects*, *tracking*, *the volume* (here, always the stage: the tracked, screen-lined space where the shoot happens; not volumetric render data as in Ch. 9 and 12, and not the capture volume of Ch. 16), and *previs* (rough pre-visualization) in a clause each.
- The **latency budget**: from a camera moving, to the tracking system noticing, to the engine rendering, to the screen displaying. Each stage has milliseconds; the total must stay inside a few frames or the picture visibly lags the camera.
- **Two truths, one image.** The real-time picture is never final; the final render (Ch. 8-10) will differ. Everyone on set needs to understand which differences are acceptable and which would mislead.
- **Frame-rate determinism:** dropping a frame on set is not like dropping one on the farm. It is visible, expensive, and happens with a crew watching.
- **Content has to be made for real time**, so assets built for offline rendering must be converted, simplified and kept in sync. That sync is the hardest data problem here.
- **Synchronization** of cameras, tracking, lighting, screens and capture: a shared clock and the consequences when it drifts.

**What this world looks like**

- A stage as a distributed system with physical parts: tracking cameras, render machines, display processors, operators, and a person whose job is to say "hold on."
- The day's rhythm: setup, tuning, shooting, and the nightly round of changes so tomorrow's scenes are ready.
- Why crew who have never heard of a scheduler are nonetheless experts in queueing.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- How to write a **latency budget** as a simple table and treat it like a cost budget.
- A way to ask of any shared asset: **what is its real-time twin, and who keeps them in step?**

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Hardware shopping lists, product comparisons, or stage construction details.
- The offline interactive previewer (Ch. 11), which is related but runs in the artist's seat, not on a stage.
- Capture processing (Ch. 16), except where it feeds live characters.
- Commercial or contractual details of any production.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **a stage, a quiet crew, and a number.** A camera operator pans left and the background lags by three frames. Nobody says anything for a second. Then the supervisor, very calmly, asks who touched the network. Establish how unforgiving the room is: unlike an artist's workstation, here the failure is observed live by the people paying for the day.

> **The story (illustrative composite):** the three-frame lag took two hours to find. The cause was mundane: a display processor had quietly switched on a smoothing mode after a firmware prompt was dismissed during lunch. Nobody had touched the network.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"We have a fast renderer; put it on a screen." The gentle humor of discovering that fast and *real-time* are different words, the second one with a deadline attached every sixtieth of a second.

### Two cultures, one stage (≈550 words)

Contrast the two traditions fairly.

- **Film pipeline:** precision, provenance, scale, patience. The answer arrives late and is right.
- **Game-engine tech:** speed, budgets, graceful degradation. The answer arrives now and is good enough.

What each does better, what each finds baffling about the other, and why neither is wrong. Include a small comparison table (priority, unit of time, how failure looks, what is sacred).

### The latency budget (≈650 words)

The core mechanism, explained with an everyday analogy (a relay race, or an airport with many handoffs) and then the real thing.

- Chain the stages: tracking, transport, evaluation, rendering, display. Give plausible milliseconds for each as an illustrative example, flagged as illustrative.
- Where budgets get eaten: network jitter, a single heavy asset, a screen that buffers one frame too many.
- **Measure end to end, not per stage.** A chain of fast stages can still be slow.
- Graceful degradation: reduce quality rather than miss a frame, and who decides what gets reduced.

> **Visual:** a stacked bar of the latency budget, with a thin sliver left for "everything we forgot".

### Bringing the film assets over (≈600 words)

Where the pipeline and the engine collide.

- Converting heavy offline assets to real-time versions: simplified geometry, baked lighting, reduced textures. What is lost and who signs off.
- **Keeping two versions honest:** when an artist fixes the offline asset, how does the live twin get updated, and how does anyone know they differ? (Back-reference scene description in Ch. 5 and provenance in Ch. 6.)
- Live characters driven by performance capture (Ch. 16): driving a rig fast enough, and the trade-off between rig quality and speed (preview of Ch. 18).
- The "looks great on the stage, wrong in the final" problem, and the discipline of a shared reference.

### Running a stage like a service (≈450 words)

The operational side, kept human.

- Monitoring and a dashboard that someone actually watches during a take.
- Change control on a shooting day: the temptation to tweak one last thing, and the freeze rule that prevents it. (Foreshadow Ch. 25, which carries a shooting-day example.)
- Overnight turnaround: changes requested at wrap must be ready by call time.
- Capturing what was shot (the stage's own records, kept apart from any "volume" of data): the stage's metadata (camera position, lens, tracking, live scene state) must be recorded so that visual effects can reproduce the shot later. Closes the loop with the rest of the book.

### Where the seam still shows (≈250 words)

An honest footnote: things that remain unsolved or uneasy: material and lighting disagreement between engine and final renderer, assets that diverge, the staffing of people who speak both languages. Keep it measured.

### Closing (≈250 words)

- Return to the three-frame lag. Show how the team would diagnose it now: end-to-end timestamps, not guesses.
- "What stuck" line: *real time isn't a faster pipeline; it's a different promise.*
- **Bridge:** the live stage drives a character through a rig. Rigs are software, and Chapter 18 treats them that way.

## 6. Details to get right

- Keep numbers illustrative and labeled so. Do not imply specific hardware or productions.
- Be fair to game-engine technology; it is a collaborator, not a rival.
- Avoid product names unless public and the point of the sentence.
- No confidential studio, client, or stage details.
- Make sure "real time" is defined as meeting a deadline every frame, not simply "fast".
