    Hair, Fur, Feathers, Cloth, Muscle and Skin

    The layers that make a creature look alive, the cloth it wears, and what to do when a perfectly accurate animal gets the note “it looks heavy.”

    Chapter 14Continues from: Chapter 1316 min readDraft

    The note from the review was three words long: “It looks heavy.”

    We had spent weeks on that animal. It was a wolf-sized creature, the giant's smaller cousin, and its muscles, skin and pelt were driven by a simulation that did exactly what a real animal's body does. Each footfall sent a ripple through the shoulder. The fur settled the way fur settles. A biologist would have approved.

    The director watched it twice and said it looked heavy. The supervisor, with a perfectly straight face, answered: “It is heavy. That's the problem.”

    I remember the silence, because I was the one who had spent a month arguing that accuracy would end all arguments about this creature. It did not end them. It was the place they started. The rest of this chapter is about what we did next, and about a sentence I now repeat to new hires: real animals are not designed to look good on camera.

    The comfortable belief

    Here is what I believed. If the anatomy is right and the physics is right, the picture will be right. Biology is the one art director nobody can overrule, so follow it and the notes will dry up.

    It is a lovely belief, and for the first few shots it held. Then we reached a trot across a snowy ridge, and the pelt rippled over the ribs with the dull honesty of a real wolf carrying a real wolf's weight. The story needed a creature that felt light, quick and slightly dangerous. Biology gave us an animal that felt like it had a mortgage.

    Nothing was broken. The simulation was correct. That was the uncomfortable part, because a bug has a fix, and an accurate simulation that looks wrong has only an opinion attached to it. So the real question was never how to make the animal accurate. It was how to make accuracy cheap to leave, so that when someone asks for something else, we can move without starting over.

    Layers, from the inside out

    It helps to think of a creature as an onion that someone has been rude to. At the center is a skeleton, which moves because an animator or a motion-capture session (Ch. 16) told it to. Around that sits muscle, then fat, then skin, then hair, fur or feathers, and over all of it, if the creature has a rider or a cloak, cloth. Each layer is pushed around by the one beneath it, and each can be simulated, shaped by hand, or both.

    The skeleton and its rig belong to Ch. 18, so I'll treat them as the engine under the floor. The other layers are where this chapter lives, and the useful thing to know about each is who owns it, how it's driven, and where artists push back.

    Notice how much of the right-hand column is about taste. That's not an accident. Every one of these layers makes a physical claim and an aesthetic one, and the physical claim is the only one a computer can check.

    Two layers deserve a word on why the simulation is optional. Muscle and skin are expensive, and for a creature seen at a distance, a library of pre-sculpted shapes triggered by the pose looks nearly as good for a tiny fraction of the compute. We simulated them for hero close-ups only, and shape-drove the rest. We argued about where the boundary sat on almost every show, and we never settled it permanently. It moves with the camera.

    Grooming: millions of strands, directed by hand

    The wolf had about two million strands of fur. Nobody placed them. A groom is the artist-directed arrangement of strands on a surface, and it is made the way a gardener works, not the way a bricklayer does. An artist paints where fur is dense, where it's short, which way it flows, where it clumps, and how its color varies from root to tip. The software then grows the millions of strands that obey those instructions.

    That makes a groom a recipe, not a pile of curves. This matters more than it sounds. If the groom were stored as two million hand-placed curves, then the day modeling changed the shape of the haunch, the groom would be wrecked and someone would spend a week rebuilding it. Because it's a recipe of guides, density maps and parameters, the same recipe is simply grown again on the new body. This is the asset thinking of Ch. 4 applied to hair: a groom has a version, a set of parameters and a clear owner, and it travels through the pipeline as a reusable thing.

    Simulate few, render many

    You cannot simulate two million strands. Or rather, you can, once, and then you will not be asked back. What we do instead is pick a few thousand guide curves, the strands that actually get simulated, and let every other strand follow its nearest guides by interpolation. The wolf had about three thousand. Three thousand are simulated; two million are along for the ride.

    The trade-off is the usual one. Interpolation is cheap and mostly lovely, and it fails in two recognizable ways. Strands between guides can cross each other and poke through the skin. And neighboring strands that follow the same guide can pull into a visible clump, like wet hair that has been told to behave. Much of a hair technical director's life is finding the places where the cheap trick shows and adding a guide there.

    Feathers are a different animal

    Everything above is about curves. Feathers are not curves. A feather has a shaft, a vane and barbs, and it sits in a layered pattern where each one overlaps the next in a particular order. Get the order wrong and the bird looks like it's wearing a shingled roof put on by a stranger.

    So feathers are mostly posed and ordered rather than simulated, with light dynamics on the loose ones at the wing tips, and the thing that matters most is collision between neighbors, far more than gravity. Hundreds of them have to slide past each other without any going through any other. I have come to think of a wing as a very small, very feuding committee: each member polite on its own, and all of them certain they should be on top.

    One more thing about all of these. A groom is judged under particular lighting, and fur that looks soft under flat light can look like wire in a low sun. So every groom was tested early in the lookdev setup, not in a neutral gray room, which is a lesson from Ch. 10 and Ch. 11.

    Making it move

    Strands move because we move guides. We simulate those few thousand with a body to collide against and a little wind, and the rest inherit it. Secondary motion, the trailing, lagging, jiggling response of fur, flesh and fabric to the main movement, is what makes the difference between a creature and a model of a creature. Animators can add it by hand, but nobody has ever matched the way a good simulation finds it for free.

    Before any of it runs, though, the simulation needs a clean rest pose: the relaxed shape of the body and its coverings with nothing pushing on them. If the groom is slightly inside the skin at rest, every frame starts wrong. We lost a weekend once to a rest pose that was off by a fraction of an inch on one ear. Everything downstream was simulating a creature wearing its own pelt a little too tightly, and the farm cheerfully computed it for hours.

    The run-up nobody sees

    The stability problem is the least glamorous and most expensive. A creature may stand in one pose at the end of one shot and be mid-leap in the next, with a cut between. Simulation cannot teleport. It needs to start from rest and run for a stretch of frames to let everything settle, and the audience never sees those frames. We typically ran a few dozen unseen frames before frame one, on every simulation, on every version.

    That is wasted compute, and an honest case for caching. The run-up is the same each time unless the animation changed, so the settled state is cached and reused, with the same versioning questions from Ch. 12: which animation was this cache made from, and is it still the current one?

    Finding the one rogue clump

    Debugging a pelt is a strange job, because the failure is one tuft out of two million. At frame 114 of one shot, a single clump of fur on the wolf's flank decided it had somewhere to be. The rest of the coat was perfect. It took an artist most of an afternoon to find it, and what found it was tooling: slow motion, a simplified display that shows only the guides, and a highlight that lights up any strand moving faster than its neighbors by a given margin. Debug views for simulation are a pipeline feature, not a luxury. We built ours after that afternoon.

    Cloth and clothing

    Cloth is hair's close relative and its more demanding one. Cloth simulation treats a garment as a thin surface that must collide with the body underneath and with itself, while obeying gravity, wind and stiffness. Chapters 12 and 13 both pointed here, so let's settle it.

    The difficulty, in pipeline terms, is that a thin surface hides every mistake in the wrong place. It needs a fine mesh, small time steps and a clean starting pose. A hero's cape is a few hundred thousand points, and not one of them may ever pass through the shoulder. When one does, you see it at once, because the cloth is what's on the outside.

    Simulate it or fake it

    Like muscle, cloth is a per-shot decision, made by distance and by how much the garment moves. A hero's cloak, in the foreground and whipping in wind, is simulated. A background extra's tunic, a hundred yards away, is baked: a pre-made animation cycle applied to the body, which is exactly the cheap end we met with the crowds in Ch. 13. Between them there is a gray zone where someone has to look at a layout and say honestly how many pixels the garment will occupy. We got that wrong in both directions. Simulated capes nobody could see cost a night each. A baked cloak in a close-up cost us a re-simulation of the whole sequence.

    A garment is an asset

    A garment, like a groom, has several parts that must travel together: a pattern layout, a fitted rest shape, and a simulation setup with its stiffness and weight. We version it exactly as we version a groom. The payoff is the same too. When the body changes, which it always does, the garment refits to the new shape through its recipe and does not get rebuilt by hand.

    Cloth is also the layer most often rerun at the last minute. The body above it changes, then the animation changes, and now the cape that was approved on Tuesday is wrong on Thursday. The cache costs and the run-up costs are the same as for fur, but cloth takes the blame, because it is last in the chain and everyone upstream gets to say “it was fine when I handed it over.”

    The same direction problem

    Cloth has its own version of “it looks heavy.” A cape that obeys physics does exactly what a real cape would do, which includes hiding the character's silhouette at the one moment the director wants to see it. The answer is the same as for the wolf: controls for stiffness, wind and, most useful, “hold this fold through the cut,” not reruns. Garments can hold a pose for a few frames and release, which is nothing a real cape does, and nothing a director has ever complained about.

    Anatomy versus art direction

    Now back to the wolf. The honest story is that we followed biology on purpose in some places and bent it on purpose in others. Weight and timing we kept close to life, because audiences feel them even if they can't say why. Silhouette, readability and emotion we bent without apology. A wolf's ears move to signal mood, and in a close-up we exaggerate them, because the camera is not a wolf.

    Sliders, not rebuilds

    What fixed the wolf was not a better simulation. It was a knob. We added a control for sag, the amount the pelt and body lag behind and sink under the movement. The fix for “it looks heavy” was to turn it down by about a fifth, which the simulation artist did in minutes and the farm re-ran overnight. No rebuild, no argument about physics. The note was answered by the next morning's review.

    Once we had one such control, we wanted all of them, and a short list of them became part of how we build every simulation:

    Knobs worth building in from day one

    Sag and weight. Stiffness. Wind strength and direction. Clump amount. Settle time. And “hold this shape through the cut.” None of these are physics. They are the places where someone will say “more like this” and where you would like the answer to be a number, not a restart.

    Who gets to say no

    Three people own pieces of the answer. The animator owns the performance, the simulation artist owns what the body and coverings do in response, and the supervisor owns the look the director asked for. On the wolf, the animator wanted the heavy footfalls, because they sold the weight of the character, and objected when we reduced sag in the pelt, because it fought the body language. The simulation artist found the compromise: reduce sag on the fur and the loose skin but not on the shoulder. The supervisor made the final call, and was right, and I'd like it noted that I was not consulted on whether this was a good idea.

    The conclusion I keep coming back to: the more accurate the base, the more convincingly you can cheat from it. A physically right simulation with a slider turned a fifth of the way down still reads as believable. A fake that started fake has nothing to hold it up when the lighting changes. Accuracy was never the destination, but it is the best place to launch from.

    The cost of a pelt

    None of this is free, and the bill comes in three forms: compute, storage and people.

    Orders of magnitude, not measurements. A strand count mostly tells you how large the memory problem will be at render time (Ch. 9).

    The surprise was which cost was largest. It was not the farm. It was artist time spent reviewing grooms, one lighting setup at a time, followed closely by the size of the caches. A few hundred gigabytes per shot version doesn't sound dramatic until you multiply by the versions a shot goes through and by the number of shots with the wolf in them. Storage was the cost we felt every week.

    We cut what we could. We simulated fewer guides on distant shots, we cached at lower resolution for review versions, and we baked cloth for anyone beyond the middle ground. We also regretted some of it: baking the cape for a mid-ground character saved a night and cost a week when the director moved the camera closer. Baking is a loan, and the interest arrives with the notes.

    One look ahead. A model that learns to predict muscle, skin or cloth motion from the rig's pose, in real time, is promising for previews, where speed matters more than fidelity. I'll pick that up in Ch. 31, as opinion rather than as something we run.

    What stuck

    Return to the review. Today, if the same note came back, “it looks heavy,” the answer would take ten minutes and no argument: someone would turn the sag control down, the overnight run would finish before breakfast, and we would look at it again over coffee. We built the same animal, but with the knob already there.

    Takeaway

    Accuracy is a starting point you can art direct from, not a destination. Make the biology available, and the overrides cheap.

    The wolf is one creature on one hill. A real show asks for the hill, and the forest around it, and a city in the next sequence, with a hundred thousand objects that a single artist still has to be able to move. That is the next chapter: Ch. 15, on vegetation and environments.
