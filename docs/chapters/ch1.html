    Anatomy of a Shot

    One two-and-a-half-second shot, nine stages, about a dozen departments, and the idea that quietly shapes everything else in this book.

    Chapter 1Continues from: Foreword13 min readDraft

    The shot was two and a half seconds long. It took us eleven weeks.

    You met him at the end of the Foreword. You've seen a shot like him a hundred times and never noticed. A huge, mossy giant walks out of a mist-filled pine forest, pauses, and turns his head toward the camera. Twenty-four frames a second for two and a half seconds makes sixty frames. Cut to the next scene. In a cinema it goes by in the time it takes to think oh, there's the giant, and nobody in the audience will give it another thought. If we've done our job, that is exactly the point.

    Behind those sixty frames sat about a dozen departments, a few dozen people, hundreds of files, and a great many hours of computer time. The shot was made about forty times, each version an earnest attempt that somebody watched and answered with “not yet.” Nobody was slacking. Nothing especially dramatic went wrong. That is simply what a shot costs.

    A note on the giant

    He is a composite. I've blended several real situations so that nothing here is confidential and everything is typical. The numbers are illustrative orders of magnitude, not audited figures. We'll follow this one shot through the whole chapter, and he'll wander back in later whenever the book needs a reminder of how much is hiding behind sixty frames.

    This chapter is a tour of how a shot travels from “we have some footage” to “it's in the movie.” Its job is to give you a map, because every later chapter on rendering, simulation, storage, or the render farm makes more sense once you know where it sits. If you come from web services, games, or data platforms, most of the vocabulary will be new, but I promise the problems will feel familiar. They have the same genes: too much data, too many dependencies, and users who change their minds.

    A few words first, since I'll lean on them throughout. A show is one film or series being worked on; I save “film” for the thing the audience sees. A shot is one continuous piece of footage, from a couple of seconds to a few tens of seconds, and it is the unit we plan, track and bill by. A department is a team of specialists, such as animation or lighting, and a TD (technical director) is the person who bridges artists and code; I started as one. A DCC (digital content creation tool) is the application an artist works in. The farm is the shared cluster of machines that does the heavy computing. A version is one numbered attempt at a shot, a retake is another go after notes, and final is the version somebody finally approves.

    One habit of this trade is worth flagging now, because Chapter 3 is built on it: every show is a new product. The giant's show had its own needs, its own deadlines and its own director, and last year's answers did not simply carry over. Hold that thought; we'll pay it off there.

    There's also one idea I want to plant early, because I spent my first year failing to see it. We'll get there. First, the picture I carried in my head, which you probably carry too.

    The belt we imagined

    When I joined my first VFX facility fourteen years ago, I assumed a pipeline was a conveyor belt. Footage goes on at one end. It rolls past a series of stations, each doing its job and handing the shot along, and at the far end a finished shot drops into a bin. Sushi-bar logic: plates go round, everyone takes what they need, nobody has to talk to anybody.

    It's a lovely picture and I'd like it back. It is wrong in four ways, and each of them is responsible for a large share of the engineering in this book. But you can't appreciate how a belt catches fire until you know what's riding on it, so let's meet the stations.

    A tour, in kitchen terms

    Think of a shot as a dish in a very busy restaurant. Not a burger: a tasting-menu plate, one of a thousand on the evening's list, touched by several cooks, and tasted by the head chef before it leaves the kitchen. The head chef sends it back more often than anyone would like. I'll use that picture to keep us oriented. There are nine stops.

    1. 1

      Turnover

      The order comes in

      Editorial sends over the plate, which is the footage that was actually filmed, along with a description of what needs to be added. “Giant enters frame left, feet planted on the forest floor, mist continues behind him.” That description is the nearest thing a shot has to a requirements document, and it is often a conversation in a room that somebody types up afterwards.

      Hands over
      The footage, the edit information, and notes on intent.
      Classic trouble
      The note says “make him feel heavy.” Specs are a sentence and a feeling.
    2. 2

      Tracking and layout

      Measuring the table

      Before anything digital can sit convincingly in a real scene, someone has to work out where the real camera was and how it moved, frame by frame. This is recovered from the footage itself, a job called matchmove. Layout then blocks the shot out in rough 3D: where the giant stands, how big he is, which way he faces.

      Hands over
      A virtual camera and a rough staging of the scene.
      Classic trouble
      This is the earliest data in the chain, so every error is inherited by everyone downstream. Get the camera slightly wrong and the giant's feet slide on the ground. Nobody can say why it looks off, but everybody can tell.
    3. 3

      Asset building

      Prep, the day before

      The giant himself, the pines, the moss, the forest floor: all of it was built earlier, separately, and not for this shot. Modelers sculpt the shapes, texture artists paint the surfaces, and riggers build the digital skeleton and the controls animators will use to move him. Each of these is an asset, and one giant gets used in hundreds of shots.

      Hands over
      A ready-to-use character or environment package.
      Classic trouble
      On Tuesday someone improves the giant's shoulder. By Wednesday, every shot containing a shoulder has an opinion about it.
    4. 4

      Animation

      Cooking the main course

      Animators perform the giant. Weight, timing, the half-second hesitation before the head turn: it is acting, done through a skeleton. The result is not video but data, a set of curves describing how every control moves across the sixty frames.

      Hands over
      Animation data that drives the character.
      Classic trouble
      “The head turn feels too fast” is a perfectly valid reason to redo it, and it cannot be measured.
    5. 5

      Character effects and simulation

      The sauce that takes forever to reduce

      Anything that should move because of the performance and the laws of physics, rather than by hand, is computed. Moss swaying on his shoulders, hair, muscle rippling under skin, mist curling around his legs, loose bark falling away. (Cloth and clothing have their own chapter, Ch. 14.) This is simulation, it is slow, and it produces the heaviest data in the entire pipeline: per-frame snapshots, called caches, of millions of moving points.

      Hands over
      Enormous cache files describing the motion.
      Classic trouble
      Simulations are touchy. Nudge the animation slightly and you can get a completely different result.
    6. 6

      Lighting

      Plating

      This is where all the work finally meets. The lighting artist assembles the giant, the forest, and the mist into one scene, places digital lights to match the real light in the plate, and decides how the image will be split into layers for later. It is also where every upstream inconsistency announces itself, because the problems all arrive at once and usually at one person's desk.

      Hands over
      A fully assembled scene, ready to render.
      Classic trouble
      Everyone's loose ends turn up on the lighter's plate. Pun intended, apologies.
    7. 7

      Rendering

      The oven

      The scene becomes images. Each frame can take hours on a single machine, so the work is spread across the farm, thousands of machines. What comes out is not one picture per frame but a stack of them: separate layers for lighting components, depth, masks, and more. You can't hurry the oven, you can't peek inside, and opening the door at the wrong moment costs you the batch.

      Hands over
      Image layers for every frame.
      Classic trouble
      A bad render costs a night. A bad render nobody notices until Thursday costs a week. Ch. 8
    8. 8

      Compositing

      The final garnish

      Compositors combine the rendered layers with the original plate. They match color, add atmosphere, grain, and lens effects, and fix whatever can be fixed more cheaply here than by rendering again. It is the last big creative lever, and the stage where the shot starts to look like a photograph.

      Hands over
      The finished image sequence.
      Classic trouble
      It is so good at patching problems that it can hide them for weeks. Quiet debt.
    9. 9

      Review and delivery

      The head chef tastes

      Each day the work is shown in a darkened room to the person with creative authority: a supervisor, and ultimately the director. This is dailies. The verdict is either “approved,” and the shot goes to final quality and into the film, or “notes,” and it goes round again. The review room gets its own treatment in Ch. 23.

      Hands over
      A decision.
      Classic trouble
      Sometimes the whole note is one word. Most of this book exists to make the “notes” branch survivable.

    Nine stops, and the belt picture carries you through the tour just fine. Now let's watch it fall apart.

    Except it isn't a line

    Here is the real structure of our giant's shot. Same stations, but drawn the way the data actually moves.

    Four things change as soon as you draw it honestly.

    Feedback runs backwards

    Say we reach compositing in week six and the compositor notices that the giant's foot casts a shadow that disagrees with the plate. The fix isn't in compositing. It may be in lighting. Or the foot may be an inch or two above the ground, in which case it's an animation problem. Notes travel upstream at speed, and every stage they pass through triggers fresh work downstream. The belt runs backwards, in pieces, and several people are standing on it.

    Nobody waits politely

    Waiting is expensive. If lighting held off until animation was final, lighting would start in week seven and the film would arrive a year late. So lighters begin on stand-ins, such as a grey blob where the giant will be, or last week's animation. Compositors start on early renders. Everything gets swapped for the real thing as it arrives. This is efficient, and it's also why the data in any given scene is slightly out of date somewhere. One of the most underrated pipeline skills is knowing precisely which version of everything you're currently looking at. Ch. 6

    Our giant's feet are the proof. Around version 23, someone finally noticed they hovered about an inch above the forest floor, and had for six weeks, through three departments and two reviews. Everyone had been looking at the mist. I know because I was one of the people looking at the mist.

    Things fan out, and things fan in

    The giant appears in about three hundred shots, so a change to him potentially touches all of them. That's fan-out. Meanwhile our one shot pulls in work from about a dozen places, in different tools, formats, and versions. That's fan-in. A shot is a meeting point, a bit like a family dinner where everyone insists they were told a different time.

    Retakes are the main course

    The first complete version of a shot is a draft. A typical final sits a few dozen versions in (the giant took about forty), and that isn't because anyone is incompetent. A film is discovered while it's being made. The edit shifts, a director sees the giant in context and wants him taller, and “taller” turns out to have consequences for four departments. A pipeline that assumes work is done once, in order, gets ground down by this. One that expects it survives.

    Aside

    Every facility has a file called something like final_final_v2_REALLYfinal.mov, and ours had several. If yours doesn't, you either have excellent tooling or haven't been asked to ship yet.

    A shot is never finished in order, and it is never finished once.

    I've come to think a pipeline isn't really about moving data from A to B. The belt does that, and the belt is easy. A pipeline is about keeping many people's work consistent with each other while all of it keeps changing. Almost every problem in this book, whether it concerns storage, rendering, scheduling, or tooling, is that sentence in a different costume.

The largest data, simulation caches and rendered layers, is made by machines. Lose the first kind and you've lost someone's week. Lose the second and you've merely lost a bill.     What stuck

    On delivery day, the giant stepped out of the mist, paused, and turned his head, feet planted at last. Two and a half seconds. Drawn honestly, he is a graph of boxes, arrows and loops, and if you'd been in the cinema you'd have taken a sip of your drink.

    For my first year I thought my job was to make the belt faster. It took me embarrassingly long to see that the belt was never the problem, since most of what happens to a shot happens between stops, in the loops. A pipeline team that makes those loops cheap, so that a note on Thursday costs hours rather than days, ends up doing more for the film than one that makes any single stage twice as quick.

    Takeaway

    Don't build for the shot that goes through once. Build for the one that comes back.
