# Chapter 25: Supporting a Show in Crunch

**Part VII: People and Process**
**Target length:** ~3,400 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 24 (upgrades are scheduled; failures are not), and Chapter 2 (peaks rule)
**Persona:** Jacob M. Miles, head of pipeline engineering (14 years in VFX, 9 at the studio); he carried the on-call pager for years before running the rotation.
**Foreword difference:** echoes #1, the fixed deadline (the release date does not move), paid off in Ch. 19.

---

## 1. Purpose

Chapter 2 said peaks rule. Chapter 20 said failure is the normal case. Chapter 25 puts the two in one room at 2 a.m. and asks what the **people** do about it.

The chapter's thesis: *Crunch doesn't need heroes; it needs rehearsed boredom. The best night is the one where the fix is dull, small, reversible and announced.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"Every change you ship during crunch is a bet placed with other people's deadline."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**What crunch is, to a pipeline team**

- The final weeks of a show: load at a multiple of normal (Ch. 2), review cycles shortening, and artists who have no slack to absorb a tool problem.
- Why a pipeline outage in crunch costs more than at any other time: up to 500 artists idle across the studio's two sites, a delivery date that can't move, and a creative team watching.
- Honest framing about long hours: the pipeline team works them too. This chapter treats sustainable crunch support as an engineering and management problem, not a badge of honor.

**The machinery of support**

- **On-call for the pipeline:** who is on, for what, with what authority; the difference between *primary*, *secondary* and *someone who knows the renderer*.
- **Triage that matches the stakes:** a short severity scale defined by artists' lost time and delivery risk, not by the engineer's sense of elegance.
- **Change freezes:** what freezes, when, who may grant an exception, and why the exception process matters more than the freeze.
- **The 2 a.m. fix:** the minimum safe procedure. Reproduce, smallest possible change, test against a real shot, roll out to a canary group, watch, announce, and keep the rollback one command away.
- **Communication:** a single status channel, plain-language updates, and a promise of the next update time even when there's nothing new.
- **The post-incident review** in a blameless format, and the trick of making it short enough that people attend.

**People and limits**

- Rotations, handover notes, and the rule that the person who fixed it at 2 a.m. does not also review it at 9.
- Burnout as a risk to the system: the tired engineer is the most likely source of the next incident.
- Why "the one person who knows" is an outage waiting for a date.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A **crunch readiness checklist** for the month before a delivery.
- A **2 a.m. change protocol** in a dozen lines.
- A habit of asking "how do we undo this?" before "how do we do this?"

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Farm-level retries and diagnosis (Ch. 20).
- Queue politics (Ch. 19).
- General incident-management theory.
- Labor-practice debates in the industry beyond a brief honest note.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **a single message at 2:07 a.m.**: a lighter reports that publishes are failing, there are about 200 artists at the second site starting in five hours (the studio has about 500 artists across two sites), and the fix is, in the engineer's mind, a three-line change. The three-line change is the most dangerous object in the building.

> **Illustrative night (chosen):** publishes fail for lighting because a path-length limit is hit after a naming change. The tempting three-line fix is to raise the limit on the shared service. I ship it straight away; it fixes lighting, then breaks compositing's reads, then the dailies media generation, about 90 artists and four hours in all. The rollback works only because someone had rehearsed it. The better version of that night: a one-line workaround for one department, a canary group, and the real fix at 9 a.m.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

Move fast, fix it, tell people later. Speed is kindness. Dry humor about the tidy confidence of the three-line change.

### The collision: the fix that broke more (≈650 words)

Tell the story of a well-meant crunch fix that cascaded: shipped fast, untested against a real shot, broke a second department, then a third. Count the artists and the hours. Own the mistake plainly.

Follow with a short list of the **usual ways crunch fixes go wrong**: untested, unannounced, irreversible, shipped by the only person awake.

### The reframe: reduce the blast radius (≈600 words)

The shift in thinking. You can't make fixes safe by making them perfect; you make them safe by making them **small, staged and reversible**. Introduce the idea of **blast radius**: how many people a mistake reaches before you notice. Everything in the chapter is a way of shrinking it.

> **Visual:** blast radius scale: single test shot, canary group, one department, one show, everyone, with a "who decides to widen the radius" label.

### What we built and what we practiced (≈800 words)

Concrete practices, each with a trade-off.

- **Freeze with a front door.** What is frozen, what isn't, and a one-page exception form that takes five minutes.
- **Staged rollout** for tools, even in an emergency.
- **Three worked one-liners, promised by Ch. 10, 17 and 18.** The stage (Ch. 17): nothing changes on the stage the week before a shoot, and a late display-driver update waits in a canary group of one machine until the shooting day is over. The rig (Ch. 18): a shoulder hotfix in crunch goes to a canary of three shots first, with the 112-shot lesson in mind. The renderer (Ch. 10): a renderer upgrade is frozen for the last six weeks of a show, and Ch. 28 holds the case file.
- **A rollback you've actually rehearsed.** Game days and fire drills before crunch, with a note on how humbling the first one was (illustrative: the first game day found the rollback took 55 minutes, not the 5 we had assumed).
- **Pre-crunch tuning:** capacity checks, dependency pinning, and a short "known weak spots" list shared with show leadership.
- **Handover hygiene** and shift design so the same three people aren't always the ones awake.
- **A lightweight status page** that artists can check before they ask. Fewer pings, more trust.

State what freezes cost: tools that could have saved time wait, and morale takes a hit when a small fix is held back.

### The people question (≈400 words)

A careful section on sustainability. How the team decides what is worth waking up for. Why the rule "escalate early, sleep first if you can" worked better for us than heroics (illustrative: after we adopted it, the number of second incidents caused by a tired fix dropped to near zero over one show). Keep it humane and not preachy. Note frankly what remains hard: deadlines come from outside, and no process fully protects people from them.

### The honest footnote (≈250 words)

What isn't solved. Some nights the right call is still a risky one. Freezes get lifted for the loudest voice. Post-incident reviews fade once the show is delivered.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the 2:07 a.m. message and what the response looks like now: smaller, slower, calmer.
- "What I'd tell my past self": *the most useful thing you can say at 2 a.m. is "let's wait until we can undo this."*
- **Bridge:** the people who answer those messages sit in a peculiar spot between art and engineering. Chapter 26 is about them.

## 6. Details to get right

- No glorifying overwork. The tone is "we learned to need less heroics".
- Keep to generalized incidents; no real show names, clients or internal systems.
- Severity and rollout practices should match widely-used engineering norms, described plainly.
- Be accurate about what a freeze does and doesn't prevent.

## 8. Decisions taken

- Candor on hours: plain and brief. The pipeline team works them too; the goal is less heroics.
- Freeze outcomes: one freeze saved a delivery (a risky upgrade deferred two weeks); one cost us (a small fix that would have saved artists an hour a day waited behind the freeze for three days). Both told in one line.
- The game-day story is the 55-minute rollback above.
- The crunch checklist is a one-off callout, not a recurring device.
- Owner of staged rollout and rollback: this chapter. Ch. 28 and Ch. 29 point here for blast radius and refer to it in one sentence.
