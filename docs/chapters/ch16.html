    From Capture to Character

    An actor's eyebrow, ten transformations, and the quiet ways a performance leaks on its way to a creature that has no eyebrows at all.

    Chapter 16Continues from: Chapter 1512 min readDraft

    “That’s not what he did.”

    The director said it quietly, in a review room, watching a close-up of the creature. Nobody argued. The animation was clean, the feet were planted, the mouth hit every word. By any number we had, the shot was correct. But the actor on the capture stage had lifted his left eyebrow a fraction before the line, a small, doubting lift, and the creature's brow had not. Everyone in the room felt the scene go slightly dead and nobody could say why.

    It took us three days to find it. There was no bug. The raw face video showed the lift clearly, four frames before the first word. Somewhere on the way to the creature, the lift had been smoothed into nothing, because the creature's heavy brow ridge was set to respond slowly, and a slow response eats a quick flick. Each step in the chain had done something reasonable.

    A note on this story

    It is a composite, like everything else in this book: I've blended a few real incidents and removed anything that could point at a show or a performer. The four frames and the three days are illustrative, but the shape of the story is as typical as they come.

    This chapter opens Part V, and with it a change of subject. Parts III and IV were about light and matter. Here we turn to the thing audiences actually watch, which is a performance. It also echoes the fifth of the Foreword's differences: software outlives its purpose, yet is expected to vanish. A capture session lasts a few days and is never repeated. The pipeline that carries it lasts years.

    The comfortable belief

    In the first pitch deck I ever saw for a creature show, there was a single arrow. On the left, an actor in a suit covered in small reflective dots. On the right, the monster. Between them, the arrow. It was a lovely arrow.

    The belief behind it is that performance is a thing you record once and apply, like pouring a drink from one glass into another. Capture the actor, put the result on the character, go home. And to be fair, for the first few seconds of a test, it does look like that. The creature stands up, turns its head, and moves like something alive. Then someone asks for the creature to be nine feet taller, with a stride twice the length, and the arrow quietly turns into a plumbing diagram.

    The chain of ten

    I now draw the arrow with ten boxes in it. I count them every time, because a chain you haven't counted is a chain you can't audit. Here they are, in order, with what each one can lose.

    Capture is the recording itself: cameras track small reflective markers on a performer's body inside the capture volume (the tracked physical space, not volumetric render data), while a helmet camera films the face. It can lose timing if the clocks drift, and it loses anything the cameras couldn't see. Cleanup removes noise and fills the holes where a marker was hidden behind an arm. It can lose genuine small motion, because small motion and noise look alike to an algorithm. Solve turns a cloud of points into a skeleton pose for every frame. It can lose accuracy quietly: it can find a perfectly confident, perfectly wrong answer.

    Retarget moves that motion onto the character, whose proportions, joints, and sometimes limb count differ from the performer's. It can lose almost everything that matters, as we'll see. Animation edit is where an animator changes the performance for the shot, and it can lose the original if the edit overwrites it. Rig evaluation runs the character's control skeleton, the rig (the subject of Ch. 18), and it can lose fidelity wherever the rig deforms differently from the performer.

    Simulation adds secondary motion such as flesh, cloth and hair (Ch. 14), and it can absorb or fight the animation it sits on. Corrective shapes are stored sculpts, called blendshapes when they hold a facial expression, that patch the places where the rig looks wrong at extreme poses. They can lose a pose entirely if nobody sculpted one for it. Geometry cache bakes the result to disk so that the farm can render it, and it can lose precision or, worse, a version. Render is the last step, where lighting and shading can bury a subtle expression in shadow.

    None of those ten is exotic. Most of them are somebody's entire department. The trouble is that each is owned by different people who judge it by different numbers, and a performance can be wrong without any one number being wrong. Everything looked fine at every step, which was the problem.

    The eyebrow, for the record, died at step four. I'm telling you the step because the habit I took from it is the only transferable lesson in this chapter: whenever data passes through several departments, draw the chain, count the steps, and write under each one what it can lose. It takes twenty minutes and it changes the arguments you have.

    Bodies: skeletons, scale and weight

    Solving a body looks easy to describe. You have a few dozen points moving through space, and you want the skeleton that best explains them. The trouble is that “best” is not unique. A shoulder marker can be explained by a raised shoulder or by a leaned torso, and the solver picks one. Usually it picks well. When it doesn't, the answer is smooth and confident and a little bit wrong, which is the most dangerous kind of wrong, since nothing flags it.

    Then comes retargeting, the central difficulty of the chapter. A human leg is a certain length. The creature's leg is, say, a third longer, with the knee bending the other way. If you copy the joint angles across, the feet slide on the floor. If you copy the foot positions across, the knees do something unsettling. The honest answer is that no arithmetic carries over weight, the sense that a body is heavy and takes effort to move. That has to be rebuilt by something that knows what the character is.

    The part viewers forgive least is contact: a foot on the floor, a hand on a railing. People who couldn't name a single joint will notice a foot that slides a centimeter. So we treat contact as a constraint, not an afterthought. Feet are marked as planted for the frames where they should be, and the retarget is allowed to bend everything else, hips, knees, even the timing of the swing, to keep them there. This costs fidelity. The creature no longer moves exactly like the actor. It moves like the actor would, if the actor were that creature and the floor were still the floor.

    That leads to the real trade-off, and it is a dial rather than a switch. At one end you are faithful to the performer, and the creature may look like a person in a costume. At the other end the creature is physically right for its body and the performer's intent thins out. Someone has to hold that dial, shot by shot, and the right person is usually an animation supervisor with a strong opinion and a recorded decision.

    The giant was the clean case. His walk cycle, three strides of a performer walking slowly across the capture stage, had to carry its footfall timing through all ten steps without a single foot sliding. It did, mostly because one person insisted the planted frames be marked at step three and honored at step nine. Footfalls were the only thing that walk had to say, and for once the chain let it say them.

    Faces: a shared vocabulary

    Faces are harder, for a reason that has nothing to do with technology. Every audience member is an expert. You have spent your entire life reading faces, and you will notice a quarter-second of wrong timing in an eyebrow that you'd never see in an elbow.

    So we don't try to move raw points on the face. We describe a performance as a set of expression controls: the brow raises, the left corner of the mouth pulls, the jaw opens. A face is a mix of those controls over time, a few hundred channels sampled at 120 frames a second. The cost of this approach is that everyone has to agree on the vocabulary: the actor's controls, the creature's controls, and the stylized character's controls all need to mean the same things. Getting that agreement is a meeting, a very long one, and it is better to have it before the shoot.

    The capture itself has its own misery. The performer wears a helmet with a small camera pointed at their face, and the helmet moves. Between takes it slips on the forehead, or someone adjusts it at lunch, and suddenly the same face reads as having a different brow position. We once spent an afternoon looking at a performer who appeared to change his mind about his own forehead between morning and afternoon. The fix is dull and effective: a quick neutral-face calibration at the start of every take, stored with the take.

    Retargeting to a non-human face forces the question, what does “the same expression” even mean? The creature has no eyebrows; it has a ridge. A doubting lift on a human is a small upward movement. On the creature we decided it should be a slight forward shift of the ridge and a narrowing of the eyes, which reads as the same doubt but shares no geometry with it. Some of that mapping can be automatic. The part that matters is authored by a person who understands both faces, and it is a piece of design, not a piece of math.

    The uncanny valley, the unsettling feeling of a face that is almost but not quite human, tends to be blamed on detail. In the data it shows up as timing and asymmetry. A real face is never quite symmetric, and a real expression does not begin everywhere at once. Smooth both of those away, as our eyebrow got smoothed away, and you get a face that is perfect and wrong.

    Keeping the original recoverable

    This is the data-engineering heart of the chapter, and where the book's earlier themes come back. The performance belongs to the performer, and the pipeline's duty is to carry it faithfully. Two habits follow.

    First, layer, never overwrite. The source performance sits at the bottom of a stack, frozen. Every change an animator makes goes into a named layer above it: “lift the brow earlier”, “soften the landing”. The final result is the stack added up, and any layer can be switched off to see what's underneath. This is the same instinct as provenance in Ch. 6: the human decision and the original stay separate from the regenerated product.

    Second, take everything immutable and traceable. A take, the recording of one run of a scene, is as precious as a film negative: named, tagged, and never altered. A solve is tied to the exact solver version and settings that produced it, and a retarget to the exact solve it came from. When someone asks “which solver made this?”, the answer is a lookup, not an investigation.

    That discipline gets tested the day a better solver arrives. Ours arrived at week twenty of a show. It was clearly better, so we re-solved all 300 takes. Of the animators' edits sitting on top of the old solves, 35 no longer lined up. The new solve had put a wrist a few centimeters from where the old one had it, and an edit that said “lift this wrist” was now lifting the wrong wrist. It took one person about a week to re-base them by hand. There was no clean answer, only a recorded one: we wrote down which takes had moved, why, and what we had done about each. We'd do it again, but we'd have scheduled it between sequences instead of during one.

    There's one more cost, which is storage. The face video from a single capture day runs to tens of gigabytes an hour, and the body data is small by comparison. All of it has to be kept, because the source is exactly the thing we promised not to lose. It is a storage and I/O problem like the ones in Ch. 7, only with a longer memory. The payoff is that takes get reused. A walk captured for one shot becomes part of a motion library, and the same library feeds the crowds in Ch. 13.

    I should say a word about machine learning. Learned solvers can speed up cleanup and give a nicer first pass at a solve, and we've tried them. But a learned solver is one more versioned dependency, with all the provenance questions that implies, and I'll leave the larger questions to Ch. 31.

    Where artists and engineers disagree

    Some animators would rather throw the capture away and animate the shot by hand. Some engineers would like exactly one trustworthy pipeline and no exceptions. They are both a little right.

    The animators have a point. Capture is a record of what a person did, not what the shot needs, and a performer who isn't a nine-foot creature doesn't always know what a nine-foot creature should do. The engineers have a point too: a path that doesn't exist yet is a path that nobody has debugged. Our middle road is that each shot is declared as one of three things, and the declaration is recorded. Capture as reference: the animator watches it and animates by hand. Capture as foundation: the animator starts from it and edits in layers. Capture as final: the performance goes straight through with minimal change. The supervisor chooses, per shot, and can change the choice later without losing anything.

    I learned the value of that choice by overruling an animator once. A shot of the creature stumbling had come out of capture looking stiff, and the animator wanted to key it by hand. I said no: schedule, and the take was good. She did it my way without comment. The shot went through four rounds of notes, and every one of them said, in some form, that it looked like a man pretending to stumble. In the end she keyed it by hand over a weekend and it was right on the first try. Her only remark, delivered very kindly, was that it had been the educational way to spend a month. She was right, and I've been grateful for the principle ever since: ask the person with the hands.

    What stuck

    Back to the eyebrow. These days, before a performance shot goes to the director, we put the raw face video next to the final result and make the differences visible: a short list of what moved, what didn't, and when. It isn't glamorous. It catches things that look fine at every step, which is exactly the thing the director's quiet sentence was trying to tell us.

    Looking back across Parts II to V, there is one principle I keep arriving at in different clothes: keep the human decision and the original separate from the regenerated product. Provenance, memory, simulation caches and now performance are all the same lesson. We'll name it properly in Ch. 30.

    Takeaway

    You can't preserve everything, so decide what you're preserving, out loud. Retargeting is translation, and translation is where the meaning leaks.

    Everything so far has assumed we have time: capture, then solve, then edit, then render, then watch in a review room. But the same performance data can drive a character live, in front of the camera, while the director is still standing there. Chapter 17 takes it to the set, where there is no time to be right, only to be fast.
