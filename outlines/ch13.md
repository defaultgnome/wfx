# Chapter 13: Crowds and Agents

**Part IV: Simulation and Procedural Systems**
**Target length:** ~3,300 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 12 (physics versus direction) and Chapter 9 (instancing and memory)

---

## 1. Purpose

Chapter 12 simulated matter. Chapter 13 simulates *decisions*. A crowd is thousands of characters, each doing something plausible, none of them individually animated by hand. The chapter follows the whole path: how an agent chooses what to do, how that becomes motion, and how ten thousand of them reach the renderer without eating the farm.

The chapter's thesis: *a crowd is a distribution problem twice over. You distribute behavior among the individuals, and you distribute the cost among the frames. Get either wrong and the army looks like a queue at an airport.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"Nobody notices a good crowd, and everybody notices the one person walking into a wall."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Mental models**

- An **agent** is a character with a small brain: it senses its surroundings, picks a behavior, and blends pre-made animation clips to carry it out.
- Two broad ways to give agents behavior: **rule-based** (state machines, decision trees, simple steering such as "avoid neighbors, seek the goal") and **learned or sampled** (motion data chosen or synthesized from a library). Most real crowds mix both.
- The **motion library** is the real asset: motion-captured clips, cycles, transitions. A crowd is only as varied as the library it draws from (Ch. 16 supplies that raw material).
- **Variation is the whole game:** body shape, clothing, props, colors, timing offsets. The eye spots repetition very fast.
- **Levels of detail (LOD):** a hero agent in the foreground, a simplified one at mid-distance, a card or a few polygons at the horizon. The pipeline picks automatically per frame.
- **Instancing:** sharing one copy of heavy data (a character's geometry) among many placements. Callback to Ch. 9, now with agents that each deform differently.
- **Directability:** the supervisor wants "a charge from the left, slowing as it reaches the wall", not an emergent surprise. Emergence is welcome only when it behaves.

**Concrete things to see**

- The path of one agent: placement, goal, a behavior choice, a blend of clips, a skinned pose, a cache, a render.
- The data funnel: thousands of lightweight "agent states" that become a few heavy renderable scenes.
- A short list of crowd bugs we all recognize: foot-sliding, collisions, synchronized walking, the one jumper.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- How to separate **simulation of intent** (cheap, runs once) from **expression of pose** (expensive, runs at render time).
- Why a cache of agent *state* beats a cache of agent *geometry*.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Game-AI pathfinding theory and its academic literature beyond a nod.
- Facial animation and single-character performance (Ch. 16).
- Cloth and hair on crowd agents beyond a note that they are usually simplified or baked. Cloth and clothing are owned by Ch. 14, which says how a costume on a hero differs from one on an extra.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **one agent.** A single background character in a battle scene who, for fourteen frames, turned and walked calmly against the charge. Nobody noticed for two weeks, until a reviewer asked who "that fellow with the shield" was. He had a name by the end of the day, and a bug number.

Pivot: multiply him by thirty thousand, and ask how you would ever review them all. (Scale: the battle's wide shots carry about thirty thousand agents; the walk-the-wrong-way agent is one of them.) *(illustrative)*

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"Crowds are just copies." A paragraph on the early assumption that you duplicate a handful of characters and scatter them, and how it lasted until the first wide shot.

### How an agent decides (≈650 words)

The "brain" section, in plain terms.

- **Sense:** what's near me, where is my goal, what's the terrain.
- **Choose:** rules or a small decision graph, with a dash of randomness so no two agents decide in lockstep.
- **Act:** pick the right clip, speed it up or slow it down to match the ground, and blend between clips smoothly.
- The key tension: the *more* autonomy you give agents, the less a supervisor can direct them. A sliding scale from fully scripted to fully emergent, with the sweet spot usually closer to scripted than my own early enthusiasm for emergence expected.

> **Visual:** a "dial" from *scripted* to *emergent* with example shot types placed along it.

### The variation problem (≈500 words)

- Building enough different-looking people from a limited set of parts: mix-and-match bodies, outfits, props and color palettes, with rules to keep combinations believable (no one wearing three hats).
- Timing offsets and personal "style" (a slouch, a stride length) assigned per agent.
- Why a library of 50 clips can feel like 5,000, and when it can't.
- The cost: every variation multiplies the assets someone must build, version and test (Ch. 4).

### From thousands of agents to one frame (≈700 words)

The pipeline-engineering core.

- **Simulate once, render many:** agent states are cached cheaply; geometry is generated at render time.
- **Instancing with deformation:** why a skinned, moving character is harder to share than a rock (Ch. 9 arriving again).
- **LOD and culling:** deciding what to build per frame, based on camera distance and visibility.
- **Handing off to lighting and rendering:** crowds as layered scenes, how they're referenced in scene description (Ch. 5), and the memory ceiling that dictated the design.
- **Interaction with other departments:** crowds that must step around a simulated wave (Ch. 12) or be hit by one. Who owns the seam? (Illustrative answer: crowds and effects, and the rule that the crowd team never edits a cache, it asks for a new one.)
- **Cloth on agents:** capes and banners on a thousand agents are baked or faked. Real cloth simulation, and the choice between simulating and shape-driving a costume, lives in Ch. 14.

Include a small table: agent distance, representation, rough cost per agent, when it switches.

### Reviewing the unreviewable (≈400 words)

How you check thirty thousand people.

- Automatic checks for the common bugs (foot-sliding, intersections, repeated poses).
- Review by sampling and by "heat map" instead of by eye.
- The supervisor's trick: stop the frame, point at any one person, and ask what they're doing. The agent had better have an answer.
- Callback to artist trust (Ch. 10): artists will accept a statistical argument only after being burned by a visible one.

### What still hurts (≈250 words)

Learned motion that is lovely until it isn't predictable, authoring effort for a new motion library, memory at the extreme wide shot, and tools that only two specialists can run. One line, deferred to Ch. 31: learned crowd motion (a model that picks or synthesizes movement from capture data) is promising, and Ch. 31 picks it up.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the lone fellow with the shield: now the system would flag him in minutes rather than weeks.
- "What stuck": *spend your complexity on the ten percent of the crowd the camera can see.*
- **Bridge to Chapter 14:** crowds are many bodies seen from afar. The next chapter goes the opposite way, close in, to what a single creature's skin, fur and muscle do.
