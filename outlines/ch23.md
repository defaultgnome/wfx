# Chapter 23: Tools Artists Actually Use

**Part VII: People and Process**
**Target length:** ~3,800 words (includes the review room section)
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 22 (making compute visible to the people spending it), and the whole technical arc of Parts II-VI

---

## 1. Purpose

Parts II through VI were mostly about systems. Part VII is about the people who sit in front of them, and it pays off Foreword difference #3: the users' product is something else, so the tool is judged by their afternoon, not by our spec. Chapter 23 opens it with the most humbling lesson an engineer learns in this industry: **you can build something correct, fast and well tested, and an artist will still route around it.**

The chapter's thesis: *Internal tools fail not because they are broken but because they are built for the workflow in the spec rather than the workflow in the room. The cure is cheap and slightly embarrassing: go and watch.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"The tool is not the product. The artist's afternoon is the product."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Why internal tools fail**

- The usual suspects: built for an imagined user, solving the engineer's version of the problem, adding a click to save a minute somewhere the artist wasn't losing one.
- **The captive-audience illusion.** Artists *have* to use your tool, so feedback looks like silence. Silence is not approval; it is a workaround in progress.
- **The "script nobody told us about" problem.** Every department has personal scripts, shelf buttons and folder habits that quietly hold the real workflow together. Replace the pipeline step they sit on and you break something you never knew existed.
- The cost asymmetry: a tool that wastes 20 seconds, used 60 times a day by 300 of our artists, is about eleven person-years of lost time a year (20 seconds x 60 x 300 artists is 100 hours a day; over roughly 250 working days that is about 25,000 hours, or about eleven person-years at roughly 2,300 working hours a person-year). Nobody files a ticket about it.

**What the day of watching teaches**

- How to **shadow an artist for a day**: what to bring (nothing), what to say (almost nothing), what to write down.
- What you see that no survey shows: the second monitor with sticky notes, the manual rename before every publish, the step done twice "just in case".
- Speed and predictability beat features. Artists forgive a missing feature faster than a surprise.
- Failure messages are UX. An error written for the engineer is a support call written for the pipeline team.

**Making tools stick**

- Fit into the host application instead of asking artists to leave it (back-reference Ch. 24 for the integration cost).
- Defaults that are right 90 percent of the time, and an escape hatch for the rest. Optional steps get skipped: if a step is not the default, assume it will not happen (Ch. 22 points forward to this).
- **Adoption without mandate:** if you have to order people to use it, find out why they'd rather not.
- A light feedback loop: a visible channel, a promise to answer, and the discipline to say no with reasons.
- Metrics that tell the truth about use (Appendix C covers the misleading ones).

**The review room (Ch. 2's fifth "what broke first" item)**

- Review is where a shot's life is decided: **dailies** (the daily review session where supervisors look at the day's new versions) set what each artist does tomorrow, so the review room is a tool artists and supervisors use more than any other.
- The review loop as a pipeline: media generated for every submitted version, queued for the session, played back color-accurately, notes captured against the exact frame, and the next version linked to the notes that caused it (Appendix B.3).
- Review capacity is a real limit: one room, a fixed number of minutes, and about 10,000 shots a year all wanting a slot. Choosing what gets reviewed is a scheduling problem with feelings.
- **Annotation:** notes drawn on the frame beat notes typed about it; the cost is that they must survive into the next version's hands.
- Planting line for Ch. 31: review formats (higher dynamic range, new display types, remote and immersive review) are a loose end the review room will inherit. Say so in one sentence, no promise.
- Planting line for Ch. 31: search and tagging of review media (finding "the shot with the green sky from last Tuesday's dailies") is a question we have, not a tool we adopted. One sentence, flagged as a question.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A one-page **shadowing checklist**.
- A habit of asking "what did you do before this tool existed?" and "what do you do when it breaks?"

**Deliberately out of scope**

- UI design theory and widget-level guidance.
- Plugin and integration mechanics (Ch. 24).
- The people who build the tools as a career path (Ch. 26).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **the day I sat behind an artist.** A tool the team had been proud of for months sits unopened while the artist does the same job by hand, with a script pulled from a folder named something like "old_stuff_DONT_DELETE". Nobody was embarrassed. Everyone was a little surprised.

> **Illustrative story (chosen):** a publish tool built over four months, with a pretty validation step, sits unused for six weeks. The compositing department keeps using a ten-line script from a folder named "old_stuff_DONT_DELETE" that renames files and skips validation. The script saves each artist about two minutes per publish, thirty-five publishes a day across the department. The tool is not wrong, it is just two minutes slower. The team copies the script's behavior into the tool; adoption goes from near zero to nearly everyone in a week. The reverse case, told in one line: a rough contact-sheet tool built by a TD in an afternoon spreads by word of mouth to three departments.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

If the requirements are written down and the tool meets them, it's done. Gentle humor on a requirements document signed by everyone except the people who would use the thing.

### The collision: where tools go to die (≈600 words)

Three short vignettes in the "what actually happened" register.

- **The tool nobody opened.** Correct, tested, unused.
- **The 20-second tax.** The small friction multiplied across a department.
- **The script nobody told us about.** A migration breaks a department's invisible glue, discovered by the loudest possible route: a delivery night.

Tie the third to Chapters 5 and 28: migrations break people, not just data. This chapter owns the full vignette of the script nobody told us about; Chapters 5, 6, 28 and 29 use a one-line callback. The illustrative delivery night: a department's home-folder script quietly fixed a color tag on every plate, a migration removed the folder, and thirty shots went out a shade off until a supervisor noticed in dailies.

### The reframe: watch, don't ask (≈600 words)

The shift in thinking. Surveys and requirement meetings record what people *say* they do. A day of shadowing records what they do. Walk through how to run one and what it turns up. Be honest that the first few feel like an intrusion, and that the artist is doing you a favor.

> **Visual:** a "workflow as spoken vs. workflow as observed" side-by-side for a single task, with the hidden steps highlighted.

### The review room (≈700 words)

Hook: one room, one big screen, fifty minutes, and a queue of versions longer than the time. Hidden belief: review is a meeting, not a system. Collision: media that wasn't ready, a color mismatch between the artist's monitor and the screen that made a note meaningless, and notes lost between the room and the artist's inbox. Reframe: dailies is a pipeline whose output is a decision. What we built (illustrative): automatic review media on every publish, a fixed playlist order agreed with supervisors, one calibrated screen standard everywhere, annotations attached to the version, and a "note to next version" link. Cost: supervisors lost the freedom to "just show one more", and the rule needed an owner. Honest footnote: review capacity is the one limit the farm can't buy away. Closing planting line for Ch. 31 on review formats, framed as speculation. Support: Appendix B.3 and App. C ("time from note to new version").

### What we changed (≈650 words)

Practical habits, each with a concrete example:

- Embed an engineer with a department for a week, regularly.
- Ship small and often, with a visible changelog artists can actually read.
- Treat a personal script as **a requirement, not a violation**: find the best ones, ask the author, promote them (cross-reference the bolt-on trap in Ch. 3).
- Write error messages for the person who sees them.
- Measure time-to-complete for a task, not clicks or feature use.

### The honest footnote (≈350 words)

What still isn't solved. Tools can't fix a bad review process, only make a good one faster. Shadowing doesn't scale to 500 artists (about 1,000 staff) across two sites, and the loudest department gets the most attention. Artists leave, and take their workflows with them.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the folder of old scripts, and what it now means: a free requirements document.
- "What I'd tell my past self": *the first user interview is the one where you stop talking.*
- **Bridge:** the tools artists use mostly live inside someone else's software. Chapter 24 is about living in that house as a guest.

## 6. Details to get right

- Respect for artists is the register. Their workarounds are intelligence, not ignorance.
- Keep numbers plausible and generic. Do not name internal tools or real departments' private workflows.
- Don't drift into UI-design theory. Stay with observed behavior.
- Be fair about the reverse failure: tools shaped entirely by one loud user.

## 8. Decisions taken

- The "I watched someone work" moment is the compositing-script story in the hook; the copy-button example (a compositor re-types a shot name nine times a day because the button copies the wrong field) folds into the 20-second tax as its one illustration, not a second watching story. One fix, an artist says "oh, you could just fix that?"
- Script-nobody-told-us-about: full vignette here, one-line callbacks elsewhere.
- The shadowing checklist is a one-off callout, not a recurring device.
- Stance: pull beats push. Mandates are allowed only after finding out why people would rather not.
