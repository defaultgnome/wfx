# Chapter 11: Interactive Previews

**Part III: Rendering**
**Target length:** ~3,200 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`

---

## 1. Purpose

Part III closes here. Chapters 8-10 covered the final renderer. Chapter 11 asks how artists can see something close to it **in seconds, not hours**, and what to do about the gap that remains.

The chapter's thesis: *A previewer is a promise that what you see is roughly what you'll get. Every disagreement between the two is a broken promise, and most of them have boring causes.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"Two renderers will always disagree. The craft is knowing where, and telling the artist."*

It is also the book's first meeting of the film pipeline with game-style real-time technology (revisited in Ch. 17).

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Mental models**

- **Why previews exist:** iteration speed. An artist who waits hours for feedback makes few decisions; one who waits seconds makes hundreds.
- **Two ways to build one:** a separate real-time (GPU, rasterization-flavored) renderer, or a progressive version of the final renderer. Explain each in plain words.
- **Progressive rendering:** an image that starts coarse and sharpens. Good for lighting, awkward for animation.
- **Sources of disagreement,** grouped for the reader:
  - **Lighting model:** shortcuts in shadows, bounce light and reflections.
  - **Materials:** the same material description interpreted by two implementations.
  - **Geometry:** subdivision, displacement, instancing and level-of-detail differences.
  - **Color:** color management and tone mapping applied (or forgotten) in one path.
  - **Features:** volumes, hair, depth of field and motion blur that the previewer fakes or skips.
  - **Scale:** the full scene doesn't fit in GPU memory (callback to Ch. 9), so the previewer shows a reduced one.
- **Defining agreement:** not pixel-identical, but *the same decisions*: if an artist approves a preview, the final shouldn't surprise them.
- **Scene description as the contract** (Ch. 5): both renderers read the same description, so disagreements can be traced to interpretation, not input.

**Concrete things to see**

- A table of the sources of disagreement, each with a symptom an artist would describe.
- A side-by-side of a preview and a final, with the differences annotated.
- A simple latency scale: how long each kind of preview takes, and what it is good for.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- Treat agreement as a **tested property**, with reference scenes and automated comparisons.
- Make disagreement **visible**: label what the previewer is faking.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Graphics-API and shader programming detail.
- Virtual production and latency on set (Ch. 17).
- Final-render noise and denoising (Ch. 10), beyond how previews borrow them.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **a lighter, and a small betrayal.** In the previewer, a shot looks perfect, and the lighter approves a dozen choices at speed. Six hours later the final render shows a reflection that was never there, and a window that glows a different color. The lighter, politely, asks which one is lying. (illustrative)

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

*A preview is just a cheaper render.* The idea that turning down quality turns one into the other. Light humor on how many "cheaper" things there turned out to be.

### Why we wanted one (≈450 words)

- The feedback loop in numbers: renders that take hours versus decisions that need seconds (call back to Ch. 1 on retakes and Ch. 2 on scale).
- What artists did without one: tiny test renders, guesswork, and crops.
- What a good previewer changes: exploration, courage, and fewer wasted farm hours (a link to Ch. 22 on cost).

> **Visual:** latency scale from "viewport" to "overnight final", labeled with the decisions each supports.

### Two designs, two kinds of lie (≈650 words)

The architectural fork.

- **Separate real-time renderer:** fast, interactive, and a second implementation of everything. Its disagreement is *structural*.
- **Progressive final renderer:** perfectly honest, much slower to feel interactive. Its disagreement is *noise*.
- A hybrid, and why it became the default for us (illustrative): GPU preview for layout and look-dev, progressive final-quality for lighting sign-off.
- The cost of two implementations: every new material or light must be written twice, or generated once and translated.
- Apply Chapter 3's criteria again, briefly, on build versus adopt for the previewer.

**Order tried (illustrative):** we started with a separate GPU previewer, regretted the two-implementations tax within two shows, tried a progressive version of the final renderer, found it too slow to feel interactive for layout, and settled on the hybrid.

### Sources of disagreement (≈650 words)

The core of the chapter, told as a short field guide, not a list in prose.

- For each class (lighting, materials, geometry, color, features, scale): a one-sentence cause and a one-sentence symptom.
- The **color mismatch** story: a simple, humbling one, since the cause is usually a missing step rather than a hard problem. Add a short **color sidebar** (about 250 words, for readers who haven't met color management): color management is agreeing which numbers mean which colors on which screen, and a preview that skips one step shows a window glowing the wrong color. The simplest case of the preview/final disagreement. Colors are named here, not taught in depth.
- The **geometry mismatch**: subdivision and displacement implemented slightly differently, leaving cracks or bulges.
- The **material mismatch:** one description, two interpretations. Why a single shared material definition pays for itself.
- What *can't* be fixed: real-time ray tracing narrows the gap but doesn't close it.

> **Visual:** an annotated preview/final pair with numbered callouts matching the field guide.

### Making them agree (≈450 words)

- **Reference scenes:** a small library that both renderers must render, compared automatically each night.
- A **tolerance** per feature, agreed with artists **in the room when the previewer was designed** (two lighters and a look-dev lead sat in the design reviews, which is why the tolerances held up; Ch. 30 cites this): this much difference is acceptable, beyond it is a bug.
- **Single-source definitions:** one shader or light description feeding both paths.
- A published **"what the preview fakes"** page in the artist's own words.
- Who owns disagreement: the previewer team, the renderer team, or the asset builder? The lane problem again (Ch. 4): who owns which fact about an asset, here, which side owns the disagreement. We settled on the asset builder first, then the team whose implementation is wrong.

### Showing the seams (≈350 words)

- Honest UI: a badge for "this effect is approximated", a toggle to switch to progressive, a one-click "send a test frame to the farm".
- Why showing the seams *raises* trust, tying back to Ch. 10.
- A short note on the cost of getting this wrong: artists who stop believing the preview and go back to waiting.

### The honest footnote (≈200 words)

What still isn't solved: complex scenes that never fit; the long tail of features approximated forever; two codebases that drift whenever the team is busy. Planting line for Ch. 31: neural previews are a question we asked and did not answer; would artists trust them within tolerance? Note that GPU and CPU renderers converging is possible (Ch. 31) but not assured.

### Closing (≈250 words)

- Return to the lighter: the same shot, with a badge reading "reflections approximated", and a lighter who sends a test frame before approving. Small, satisfying scene.
- "What stuck": *the previewer doesn't need to be right. It needs to be honest about how wrong it is.*
- **Bridge to Part IV:** rendering shows what the world looks like. The next part asks how it moves, burns, floods and falls apart. Chapter 12 starts with water, fire and destruction.
- **Part III wrap (one sentence):** four chapters, one theme: a renderer that is correct, fits, converges and previews honestly, and the pattern under all of it is that trust is built by never surprising the artist.

## 6. Details to get right

- Keep it vendor- and API-neutral. Say "GPU", not a graphics library.
- Don't claim real-time ray tracing closes the gap; say it narrows it.
- Use orders of magnitude for latency; no precise benchmark claims.
- Keep invented details generic: no show names or internal tool names.
- Be fair to game-engine tech: the point is different constraints, not inferiority.
