## 1. Purpose

Part V opens here. Parts III and IV dealt with light and matter. Chapter 16 deals with the thing audiences actually watch: **a performance.** An actor moves, speaks and emotes on a stage in a suit or in front of cameras, and that performance must arrive, recognizably theirs, on a creature with a different skeleton, different proportions, and no human face.

The chapter's thesis: *a performance is data that passes through many hands, and every handoff is a chance to lose the part that mattered. The engineering job is to decide, explicitly, what must survive.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"Retargeting is translation, and translation is where the meaning leaks."*

It also sets up the rest of Part V: real-time use of the same data on set (Ch. 17), and the rigs that carry it (Ch. 18).

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Mental models**

- Capture is a **stack of exactly ten transformations**, each with its own error. The one list, reused wherever it appears in this chapter: (1) capture, (2) cleanup, (3) solve, (4) retarget, (5) animation edit, (6) rig evaluation, (7) simulation, (8) corrective shapes, (9) geometry cache, (10) render.
- **Body and face are different problems.** Body capture solves a skeleton from markers or sensors. Facial capture must describe something subtler: a shared vocabulary of expressions, and how to map it onto a face that may not resemble the actor's.
- **Retargeting** is the central difficulty: moving motion between characters with different proportions, joint structures, and (for creatures) different numbers of limbs. Foot contact, weight, and intent do not transfer by arithmetic.
- **Cleanup and the human in the loop.** Raw capture is noisy, occluded, and occasionally wrong. Automated solving gets most of the way; artists finish.
- **Fidelity is a choice, not a goal.** Sometimes the director wants the actor exactly. Sometimes they want the actor *exaggerated*, or deliberately *not*. The pipeline must support editing without destroying the source.
- **The source performance is precious and must stay recoverable.** Every edit layers on top of it, never overwrites it.

**What this world looks like**

- Capture stages (the **capture volume**, meaning the tracked physical space, not volumetric render data from Ch. 9/12 or the LED stage of Ch. 17), head-mounted cameras, and the data size of a single take, in plain illustrative numbers: 120 frames per second, a few hundred channels, tens of gigabytes per hour of face video.
- Why the same take may be used in many shots, and why takes are named, tagged and trusted like film negatives.
- Evaluation: how do you measure whether a performance survived? (Spoiler: mostly by watching, with a few helpful numbers.)

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A habit of drawing the **transformation chain** for any data that passes through multiple departments, and marking which steps are lossy.
- A way to think about **round-tripping**: can you go from the final result back to the source, and should you?

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Camera and sensor hardware comparisons, or product recommendations.
- Muscle, skin and anatomical simulation (Ch. 14) and crowds (Ch. 13); mention only where they pick up the animation.
- Real-time use on set (Ch. 17) and rig engineering (Ch. 18).
- Machine-learning solving in depth. One honest paragraph here (learned solvers can speed up cleanup but are one more versioned dependency, Ch. 6); the cross-cutting questions go to Ch. 31.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **a small, human moment from a review session.** The director watches a creature's close-up and says, quietly, "That's not what he did." The animation is technically perfect. The actor's eyebrow, though, lifted a fraction before the line and the creature's brow does not. Nobody can find the bug because there isn't one. A detail was lost at step four of ten (retarget), and each step looked fine on its own.

Establish the stakes: the audience never sees the data, only whether it feels like a person.

> **The story (illustrative composite, flagged as such):** the actor's left eyebrow rose about four frames before the line; the retarget map smoothed it away because the creature's brow ridge has a slower response. It took three days and a side-by-side of the raw face video to find, because every step's numbers looked fine.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"Capture the actor, apply it to the character, done." Light humor on how clean that looked in the first pitch deck, with a single arrow from actor to monster.

### The chain of ten (≈650 words)

Walk the ten-step list from section 2 end to end, one or two sentences per step, in that order: capture, cleanup, solve, retarget, animation edit, rig evaluation, simulation, corrective shapes, geometry cache, render. Name what each step can lose (timing, contact, subtle motion, scale). Introduce vocabulary inline: *marker*, *solve*, *rig*, *blendshape* (a stored expression shape), *retarget*.

> **Visual:** the chain as a horizontal strip with a "what can leak here" caption under each step.

### Bodies: skeletons, scale and weight (≈550 words)

- Solving a skeleton from points in space, and why the answer is never unique.
- Retargeting a human onto a creature with different leg length: foot sliding, floating, and the loss of apparent weight.
- Why **contact** (feet on floor, hand on object) is the thing viewers forgive least, and how pipelines treat it as a constraint rather than an afterthought.
- The honest trade-off: faithful to the actor, or physically right for the character? Usually a dial, and someone has to hold it.

### Faces: a shared vocabulary (≈650 words)

- Why faces are harder: viewers are experts at them.
- Describing a performance as a set of expression controls rather than raw points, and the cost of agreeing on that vocabulary across actors and characters.
- Head-mounted cameras, stabilization, and the quiet misery of a camera that moved on the actor's head between takes.
- Retargeting to a non-human face: what "the same expression" even means on a creature with no eyebrows. Artist-authored mapping versus automatic.
- Where the uncanny valley actually shows up in the data: timing and asymmetry, not detail.

### Keeping the original recoverable (≈450 words)

The data-engineering heart of the chapter, and where the book's earlier themes return.

- **Layering, not overwriting:** source performance, plus named edit layers on top. Animators change a layer; the source stays frozen. (Provenance as in Ch. 6.)
- **Versioning performance data:** takes, solves, and retargets are immutable and traceable to the exact solver and settings that made them.
- **Re-solving later:** when a better solver arrives mid-show, can you reprocess all takes, and what happens to the animators' edits sitting on the old solve? Illustrative case: a better solver arrives at week 20; 300 takes are re-solved, 35 animator edits no longer line up, and a person-week goes into re-basing them. No clean answer, only a recorded one.
- Storage and I/O of capture data in bulk (back-reference Ch. 7).
- **Capture libraries:** takes are reused across shots, including as the motion library that crowds consume (Ch. 13).

### Where artists and engineers disagree (≈350 words)

A short, fair section. Animators sometimes want to throw the capture away and animate by hand; engineers want a single trustworthy pipeline. The pragmatic middle: capture as reference, as foundation, or as final, decided per shot and recorded. The narrator learned this by overruling an animator once and regretting it; the animator's verdict on the overruled shot is the book's one use of "the educational way" (said kindly, and the narrator is the joke).

### Closing (≈250 words)

- One-line callback to the giant: its walk cycle, captured from a performer on the stage, is the clean example of a chain where footfall timing had to survive all ten steps.
- Return to the eyebrow. Show what the team would now do: a side-by-side of source and result, with the differences made visible before the director sees it.
- Name the recurring principle of Parts II to V once here: *keep the human decision and the original separate from the regenerated product.* (Tagged again in Ch. 30.)
- A short "what stuck" reflection: *you can't preserve everything, so decide what you're preserving, out loud.*
- **Bridge:** the same performance data can drive a character live, in front of the camera. Chapter 17 takes it to the set, where there is no time to be right, only to be fast.

## 6. Details to get right

- Treat actors and animators with respect: the performance belongs to the performer, and the pipeline serves it.
- Keep numbers plausible and generalized (frames per second, channels, hours of capture), not tied to any specific production.
- Vendor-neutral on hardware and software.
- No confidential show or actor details; the story is a composite, flagged as such.
- Define each term on first use; many readers come from backend or games and know "skeleton" but not "retarget".
