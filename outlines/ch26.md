# Chapter 26: Pipeline TDs: The Bridge Role

**Part VII: People and Process**
**Target length:** ~3,200 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 25 (the people who answer the 2 a.m. message)
**Persona:** the narrator (Jacob M. Miles, head of pipeline engineering, started as a pipeline TD, 14 years in VFX, 9 at the studio) writes as someone who hired for these roles.

---

## 1. Purpose

Everything in this book depends on a role that barely appears in other industries. A **pipeline TD** (technical director) is part engineer, part artist's translator, part firefighter, and often the only person who can read both a stack trace and a lighting complaint. Chapter 26 asks how to hire, grow and keep them.

The chapter's thesis: *The bridge role is the most valuable and the most fragile job in a facility. It is valuable because it connects two cultures, and fragile because both cultures tend to see it as the other one's problem.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"The job isn't half art and half code. It's fluency in both, and patience for the gap."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**What the role is**

- A plain definition for an outsider, with a day-in-the-life sketch: a morning of triage, a midday session sitting with an artist, an afternoon writing a small tool, an evening in review.
- The spectrum: from show-embedded TDs close to the artists, to pipeline developers closer to platform engineering. Different jobs with shared vocabulary.
- Why the role exists: the gap between "what the artist needs" and "what the system can do" is wide, constant and costly. Someone has to stand in it.
- Where it overlaps with, and differs from, a software engineer, a technical artist (in games) and a support engineer.

**Hiring**

- Two common sources: artists who learned to code and engineers who learned to see. Strengths and blind spots of each.
- What to look for that a résumé hides: curiosity about the other side, comfort with ambiguity, and how they explain a problem to someone who doesn't share their vocabulary.
- Interview ideas: a debugging conversation with a pretend artist, reading someone else's messy script, a small-scale "explain this to a lighter" exercise. Avoid trivia and whiteboard puzzles.
- The honest market reality: the pool is small, and everyone is hiring from it.

**Growing**

- Career paths that don't force a choice between management and a narrow specialty: deep technical, show-facing lead, platform owner, and cross-department architect.
- Mentoring across the gap: pairing engineers with artists, and artists with code review.
- Making time for learning when the job is permanent triage.
- Recognizing invisible work: the problem that never happened because someone fixed it quietly.

**Retaining**

- Why TDs leave: unrecognized work, unsustainable crunch (Ch. 25), being the "only one who knows", and no visible path forward.
- What helps: a clear career path, pay that matches the engineering market, visibility with leadership, and permission to say no.
- The bus factor as a people issue: spreading knowledge is also a retention tool.
- Respecting that some people will leave to the games, tech or startup world, and designing for graceful departures and boomerangs.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A short **role profile** the reader can adapt, covering a pipeline TD, a show TD and a pipeline developer.
- A set of **interview prompts** that test bridge skills.
- A habit of asking "who is standing in the gap on this project, and do they know it?"

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Compensation figures or named individuals.
- Detailed HR policy or union matters.
- Art-department career paths in general.
- The research role (Ch. 27).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **a job posting.** The author tries to write one for the role and discovers every draft sounds like three different jobs, each of which would scare off the right candidate. Then a quick scene of the actual person: it's late, a lighter and an engineer are describing the same problem in two different languages, and the TD says one sentence that makes both of them nod.

> **Illustrative portrait (composite, chosen):** a TD I'll call "the one who read the shot." A lighter and an engineer argue for twenty minutes about a flicker; she asks to see the contact sheet, says "it's the cache, not the light", and both nod. She also keeps a private list of every home-folder script in her department (the one at the heart of Ch. 23), and reads the orphaned tool's logs for fun. None of it was in her job description. She later becomes the queue owner in Ch. 19's sense: the person who knows whose job is waiting and why.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"Hire engineers; artists can file tickets." Or the reverse: "hire artists who script." Light humor on the posting that asks for twelve years of experience in a tool that is eight years old.

### The collision: the gap between two teams (≈600 words)

A short story of what happens without the bridge. A feature built perfectly to specification that no one in lighting can use; a complaint from artists that engineering never listens; an engineer who thinks artists never say what they want. The same facts, two stories, and a missing translator.

Follow with the **ways the role is mistreated:** treated as support, as a stopgap, or as a promotion for the best artist (whom we then lose from the art side while the engineering side is still being learned).

### The reframe: a profession, not a gap-filler (≈600 words)

The shift in thinking. The role is not what you get when you can't afford both an engineer and an artist; it is the skill of making the two understand each other. Fluency is a craft with its own growth path. Treat it as such in titles, career paths and budgets.

> **Visual:** a spectrum diagram from "art-facing" to "engineering-facing", placing typical roles along it, labeled as a model rather than an org chart.

### What we do about hiring, growth and keeping (≈850 words)

Three short subsections, each with concrete practice and trade-off.

- **Hiring:** the interview that looks like a real conversation; why trivia failed (in my own early hires, which I got wrong more than once); paying attention to how candidates talk about artists.
- **Growing:** pairing programs, rotation through shows, a career path with visible steps, shared on-call (back-reference Ch. 25), and time protected for learning.
- **Retaining:** recognize invisible work, reduce the lone-expert risk, honest conversations about crunch, and being comfortable with people moving on.

> **Illustrative practice (chosen):** my first three TD hires were chosen on trivia quizzes and two were wrong for the seat. The fix was an hour-long conversation with a pretend lighter who has a flickering shot; the best hire that followed was a compositor who already ran her department's scripts. We paired every new TD with an artist for their first month, and I still count the departures as my own misses.

Name the cost of each: slower hiring, more mentoring, and a career path that has to be argued for every budget cycle.

### The honest footnote (≈300 words)

What still isn't solved. Pay gaps with other industries. The invisibility problem. The hiring pool is small and cyclical. Some of the best hires I made came from places I wouldn't have looked (a compositor who ran the department's scripts, a game-tools engineer), and some of our best people left, which I own.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the job posting and show a draft that is honest.
- "What I'd tell my past self": *the person who understands both sides is rare; treat them as infrastructure and as a person.*
- **Bridge:** one of those bridges leads toward research, new papers and prototypes arriving at the door. Chapter 27 is about letting them in, and about saying no.

## 5. Humor and tone opportunities

- The job posting that wants an artist, an engineer, a diplomat and a night owl.
- The skill that is described, universally, as "just a script".
- The person who is the only one who knows, who has thus never taken a holiday.
- Two groups describing the same bug with such different words that both are right.

Keep the target the author's own early job descriptions. Never the TDs, artists or engineers.

---

## 6. Details to get right

- No named individuals and no salary figures.
- Don't rank art over engineering or the reverse. Respect for both sides is the register.
- Avoid HR-speak ("talent", "resources", "leverage") in running prose.
- Be careful with claims about industry-wide hiring; present them as the author's experience.
