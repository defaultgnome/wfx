# Chapter 30: What We'd Do Differently

**Part VIII: Hard-Won Lessons**
**Target length:** ~3,200 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapters 28-29 (the bills for earlier decisions)

---

## 1. Purpose

This is the most candid chapter in the book, and the reader should feel the author stop being careful. Chapters 28 and 29 told specific stories of things going wrong. Chapter 30 steps back and asks, with the whole book in view: **knowing what we know now, what would we change?**

The chapter's thesis: *most of our expensive mistakes were not bad technical choices. They were good choices made with a wrong assumption about people, time or scale, and kept past their date.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"We were rarely wrong about the technology. We were often wrong about how long it would stay right."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**A shortlist of lessons, each grounded in earlier chapters**

- **Decide in writing, review on a date** (Ch. 3). We did write decision records, but too late: the renderer record in Ch. 8 was reconstructed after the fact, and the arguments it should have prevented had already repeated themselves.
- **Know your data before you design for it.** Counting objects, not just bytes, early (Ch. 2, 7).
- **Make provenance cheap from day one** (Ch. 6). Our insistence on being able to reproduce an old shot was right; we were late on the cost, and paid for it as an archaeology project eighteen months in.
- **Negotiate "correct" with the people who use it** (Ch. 8, 10, 11): artist trust is a feature. Cite Ch. 11 here: artists in the previewer design reviews caught disagreements with the final look that engineers had called acceptable.
- **Treat tools as products, with owners and users** (Ch. 23, 24).
- **Plan failure as the normal case** before the first big night (Ch. 20).
- **Make cost visible earlier** (Ch. 21, 22).
- **Invest in the bridge role** sooner (Ch. 26).
- **Kill ideas faster and more kindly** (Ch. 27).
- **Keep the human decision separate from the regenerated product** (the pattern of Ch. 4, 12, 15, 16 and 18): the lesson from the middle of the book that nothing else here carries.

**Perspective**

- Which regrets are about technology, which about process, and which about people. (Expect the last two to dominate.)
- Why hindsight is unfair: the facts we have now were not available then. Distinguish *bad decisions* from *decisions that aged*.
- What we would **not** change, and why: choices that looked wrong and turned out right.
- Where two lessons contradict (move fast vs. write it down): the chapter says so plainly and resolves it with "write it down after the first week, not before".

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Blame, named individuals, or any confidential detail about specific shows or clients.
- Re-explaining chapters; each lesson points back.
- Predictions (Ch. 31).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈250 words)

Open with a **hypothetical phone call:** the author is handed a time machine that goes back exactly one meeting, and has to choose which one. Funny, then pointed: the surprise is how many candidate meetings there are, and how few of them were about technology.

*(illustrative)* The meeting chosen: the one, early in the renderer decision (Ch. 8), where a room of reasonable people agreed on a direction, nobody wrote down why, and everyone left with a slightly different memory of what had been decided. Technically uneventful. Cost: months of re-argument.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"With more experience we would have chosen better tools." A gentle correction: most of the tools were fine.

### The regrets, grouped by what they were really about (≈1,500 words)

Three groups, each with its own sub-heading, two or three short stories and a back-reference. Prose, not a list. The thesis is stated here as a claim about our own history, not a rule.

**Assumptions about scale** (≈450 words)
Things sized for the average show instead of the monster shot or the crunch week (Ch. 2, 7, 19). A story of a system that worked beautifully until the one night it mattered.

*(illustrative)* The regret: the review-room previews (Ch. 23) sized for a normal week, which fell over during the week of a big delivery, a day before it mattered.

**Assumptions about people** (≈550 words)
Tools built from specifications instead of from watching someone work for a day (Ch. 23). Artists' intuition dismissed as nostalgia (Ch. 10). Talent grown too slowly in the bridge role (Ch. 26). The author is the target here.

*(illustrative)* The regret: a lighting tool we built from a specification and shipped to a room that had been using a spreadsheet for years. The spreadsheet had been the specification all along.

**Assumptions about time** (≈500 words)
The temporary solution, the migration plan that assumed an end (Ch. 28), the debt we assumed we'd get to (Ch. 29), the research prototype promoted too early (Ch. 27).

*(illustrative)* The regret: the decommission date for the old scheduler that we put in a slide deck as a promise.

### What we got right by accident or by stubbornness (≈400 words)

Fairness section. Two or three decisions that were criticized at the time and held up: insisting on being able to reproduce an old shot (Ch. 6), letting the old renderer finish shows already in flight (Ch. 28), and keeping the compare-both-ways habit on real shots (Ch. 5, 8). Be honest about how much was luck. Cross-reference: Ch. 6's insistence was right and late in cost; say so here and in the lessons above.

### The advice we'd give a new pipeline team (≈450 words)

A short, plain-spoken set of principles, framed as the longer version of the closing lines of Ch. 20 to 27. Resist a numbered list in the prose; a closing callout with five or six lines is fine.

- Write down what you decided and what would change your mind, in the first week.
- Watch someone work before you build for them.
- Count things; plan for the worst night, not the average one.
- Pay debt in installments and keep the ledger public.
- Hire and keep the people who sit between art and engineering.

### Closing: What stuck (≈250 words)

A humble note: this chapter will date too, and the author expects to disagree with parts of it in five years. What stuck: *the goal isn't to stop making mistakes; it's to make new ones.* Decision: no extra personal note beyond that line; the personal sign-off belongs to Ch. 31. Bridge to Ch. 31: from regrets to bets.

## 6. Details to get right

- Candor, not self-flagellation. The tone is "we fumbled our way here", not confession for effect.
- Stories are generalized and anonymous; the details above are illustrative.
- Don't turn it into a list of platitudes; each lesson must point to a story told earlier.
- Be careful with the "what we got right" section: avoid heroic framing (STYLE.md section 8).
- Keep the decision-records and provenance reconciliation consistent with Ch. 3, 6 and 8.
