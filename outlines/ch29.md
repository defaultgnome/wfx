# Chapter 29: Technical Debt in a Pipeline With No Downtime

**Part VIII: Hard-Won Lessons**
**Target length:** ~3,400 words
**Tone reference:** see `STYLE.md`
**Continues from:** Chapter 28 (migrations as dramatic change; debt as the slow kind)

---

## 1. Purpose

Chapter 28 was about the big, scheduled changes. This chapter is about the small, endless ones: the shortcuts taken in crunch, the scripts nobody owns, the code that works and that nobody dares touch. And it asks the question with a film twist: **how do you refactor something that a thousand people are using right now?**

The chapter's thesis: *in a pipeline with no downtime, debt cannot be paid in a lump sum. It has to be paid in installments, in the open, while the building is occupied.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"You can't close the restaurant to fix the kitchen."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**A way of thinking about debt**

- Why pipeline debt accrues faster than product debt: fixed deadlines, shows that start and end, and fixes that must ship at 2 a.m. (Ch. 25).
- That some debt is a **deliberate loan** (a shortcut taken on purpose, with a plan) and some is **accidental** (nobody noticed it was a loan). The line between them: if someone wrote down the plan to repay it, it was a loan; if the only record is a comment saying "temporary", it was accidental. They need different handling.
- How to tell debt that is costing you from debt that is merely ugly. Test: does it slow people, scare people, or break things?
- The **show cycle** as a refactoring calendar: the gaps between shows are the only semi-quiet time, and they are short.

**Ways of refactoring while occupied**

- Change behind a switch: ship new code dark, enable by show, shot or user group, and keep an instant way back. (The staged-rollout and rehearsed-rollback mechanics belong to Ch. 24 and Ch. 25; this chapter applies them to debt and does not re-teach them.)
- Growing the new path around the old one until the old one has nothing left to do (the "strangler" pattern, named second).
- Tests that capture what the system does today, because the existing behavior is the specification (echo Ch. 28).
- Using provenance (Ch. 6) and dailies output to prove "nothing changed" on real shots. The compare-both-ways pattern is the one from Ch. 5 and Ch. 8.
- Small, boring, frequent releases over heroic rewrites; link to Ch. 18 (rigs as software) for versioning and testing discipline.

**Organizational practice**

- Making debt visible: a ledger, an owner, an interest estimate in artist-hours.
- A budget for paying it down that survives crunch (a fixed fraction of time, protected).
- Knowing when to **declare bankruptcy**: delete the thing instead of fixing it.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Language-level refactoring techniques and code examples.
- Whole-system migrations (Ch. 28).
- Tooling or product recommendations.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **the file nobody touches.** It is the Ch. 3 tool, the orphaned one that quietly runs the facility, now nine years old, written by someone who left. Everyone knows its name. New hires get warned about it on their first day, in the same tone as a stove. It works, and the person who understood it is gone. Then the twist: it is on the critical path for every shot.

*(illustrative)* What replacing it would have cost: an estimated six months of two engineers, plus a freeze window that never appeared on any show's calendar. The tool, meanwhile, cost nothing to run, which is how stoves get their reputation.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"We'll clean it up after delivery." A fond look at the sentence. After delivery there is another delivery.

### Where pipeline debt comes from (≈550 words)

A short, honest taxonomy told through stories, not lists:

- Crunch shortcuts that became features.
- Per-show hacks that quietly became the default (the Ch. 3 bolt-on trap, again).
- Integration glue that tracks every DCC upgrade (Ch. 24).
- Data formats and naming conventions that outlived their reasons (Ch. 4, 5).
- Knowledge debt: the thing only one person understands (Ch. 26).

Name the cost in people's time: the habit of working around it is the interest payment.

### Why you can't stop the world (≈400 words)

The constraint that makes this different. Scale of use (about a thousand staff, about five hundred of them artists, and a million tasks a night; see Ch. 2 and Ch. 19), no maintenance window that isn't someone's deadline, and artists for whom "the tool changed" is a work stoppage, not an inconvenience.

Include the asymmetry: a regression costs hundreds of people an afternoon; a refactor that prevents one pays back slowly and invisibly.

### How we change things while people are using them (≈700 words)

The mechanism section, as short stories rather than a catalog. Ch. 24 and Ch. 25 own rollout and rollback; this section only shows how they apply to debt.

- **Dark launch and gradual rollout**, by show or by volunteer group; the "friendly show" that agreed to go first.
- **Shadow running:** new code computes the answer alongside the old and logs the difference without anyone seeing it.
- **Characterization before change:** record what it does, then change it, then compare.
- **Carve one behavior at a time off the old path** (the strangler, in one paragraph).
- **Always keep the door open:** the rollback that was actually tested, not just planned (tie to Ch. 20 on failure as normal).

> **Visual:** a diagram of the strangler: traffic share old vs. new over weeks.

### Making debt visible and paying for it (≈550 words)

The organizational half. A **debt ledger** with owner, user count, artist-hours per month lost, and a "touch it at your peril" rating. The protected budget: why it must be fixed in advance or crunch eats it.

*(illustrative)* The one time it held through a crunch: a fixed ten percent of each engineer's week, written into the show's plan before the show started, defended by a producer who liked the ledger because it had numbers in it.

A short, frank passage on selling it upward in language that matters (artist-hours, risk to delivery, cost; see Ch. 22 on making cost visible).

### When to delete (≈350 words)

Sometimes the right answer is to remove it. Features with three users, tools replaced twice over, compatibility shims for software nobody runs. The **sunset habit**: announce, warn, brown-out (break it briefly on purpose to find who cares), then remove. (Deprecation as a product practice is Ch. 24's; this is the version for debt.)

*(illustrative)* The worst well-meant cleanup: removing an "unused" export option that turned out to feed one department's nightly report. Found at 7 a.m. by the brown-out we had not run.

### The honest footnote (≈200 words)

Some debt stays. A few stoves remain. What we chose to live with and why that was a decision, not neglect.

### Closing: What stuck (≈200 words)

Return to the file nobody touches. What happened to it: it was fenced in rather than rewritten. A test harness now records what it does, an owner is named in the ledger, and it has a small plaque in the form of a comment at the top. It still runs every shot. What stuck: *the goal isn't a clean pipeline; it's one where you know where the mess is.* Bridge to Ch. 30: if we kept the mess visible, what else would we now do differently?

## 6. Details to get right

- Keep it about practice, not code. No snippets, no framework names.
- Don't shame the people who took the shortcuts; the shortcut usually saved a delivery.
- Be specific about safeguards: a rollback that has never been run is a wish.
- Numbers (users, artist-hours) are illustrative orders of magnitude.
- Same facts as elsewhere: the stove is the Ch. 3 tool at nine years; scale is a million tasks a night.
- Use US spelling.
