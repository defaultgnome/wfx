## 1. Purpose

Part VI opens here. Parts III to V described the work that goes onto the farm: renders, simulations, crowds, captures. Chapter 19 asks how that work gets *onto the machines* and who decides what runs first.

One framing note, stated once: this chapter describes the *old* scheduler, the one we ran for most of the pipeline's nine years, whose fairness rules lived unwritten in its code and in a few people's heads. Writing the policy down in a page is what the later replacement eventually forced on us (the story is told in Chapter 28).

The chapter's thesis: *A scheduler is not a piece of software that assigns jobs to machines. It is a machine for making decisions about fairness, and every setting in it is a policy someone will argue about.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"The queue is the only place where every show, every department and every deadline meet, so it is where every disagreement ends up."*

It also sets up Chapter 20: once you can start a million tasks a night, some of them will fail.

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**How a farm scheduler thinks**

- The vocabulary, defined in a clause: **job** (a request, about 240 tasks, so a million tasks a night is roughly 4,000 jobs; this is the one chapter that uses "jobs" in its title, and the only place the word is defined), **task** (one unit of it, and the unit the rest of the book counts), **slot** (a share of a machine), **pool** (a group of machines or users), **priority**, **preemption**.
- Why matching tasks to machines is the easy part. The hard part is deciding *whose* task goes next.
- Resource shapes: tasks differ in memory, cores, GPU need, licenses, and which storage they touch. A scheduler is packing, not just queueing. A rig-heavy animation task, for example, costs far more to evaluate than its file size suggests (the handoff from Chapter 18). One line also plants a seed for Chapter 31: a scheduler built around CPU frames has to learn new shapes when the unit of work changes (a GPU task, say), and that is a policy question too.
- The scheduler's own scaling limit: the system that tracks millions of tasks becomes a bottleneck for the work it manages (the Chapter 2 foreshadowing).

**Fairness is a policy, not a feature**

- Strict priority, first-come-first-served, and weighted shares, each with a plain-language example and its failure mode.
- **Fair share with decay:** shows that used a lot recently wait a little. Why it feels right and where it surprises people.
- **Preemption:** killing a running task to make room. The trade-off between responsiveness and wasted work (a cost that Chapter 20 turns into numbers).
- **Reservations and the deadline rush:** how a show near delivery earns a bigger slice, and who approves that.
- The honest limit: no policy is neutral. The queue encodes the studio's values.
- The old scheduler's secret: the rules that made it work (who borrows, who decays, who gets a quiet boost at delivery) were never written anywhere. They were behavior, discovered by arguing about it.

**The politics**

- Why "bump my priority" requests arrive in a steady stream, and why a priority scale of ten levels becomes a scale of two (everyone is 10).
- Making the rules visible: a **queue dashboard** artists can read, and the shift in tone when people can see why they are waiting.
- Governance: who owns the shares, how they change, and a regular review rather than a hallway negotiation.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A way to write down a fairness policy in a page.
- A short list of signals to watch: wait time by show, utilization, wasted work from preemption, and scheduler latency.
- The habit of asking "what behavior does this setting reward?"

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Failure handling, retries, checkpointing (Ch. 20).
- Cloud capacity and bursting (Ch. 21).
- Cost allocation and chargeback (Ch. 22).
- Product comparisons of schedulers; keep to concepts.
- Migrating schedulers (Ch. 28 covers it; this chapter only plants the setup).

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open on **a message.** At 9:40 a.m., a message from a supervisor: *"Can you bump my show up? Just for today."* The author knows that three other supervisors have sent the same message and that everyone's request is reasonable. Volume appears only in passing ("a farm that starts a million tasks a night"); the full reveal of what that number means is saved for Chapter 20.

Then the dry observation: the scheduler is the only system in the building where all the productions are in the same room.

> **Illustrative detail:** the "just for today" request came from the supervisor of a show three weeks from delivery. "Today" lasted eleven days, and the boost was only noticed when it quietly outlived the show's delivery, still sorting a finished show ahead of everyone else.

### The comfortable belief (≈200 words)

A scheduler is a matching problem: tasks on one side, machines on the other, fill the gaps. Light humor about the first version, which was essentially a list sorted by a number and a very confident cron job. Its fairness rules were never written down; they were whatever the code happened to do, and everyone believed the code was fair.

### What a scheduler actually decides (≈600 words)

Walk through a single task's life: submitted, waiting, matched, started, finished. At each step, name the decision being made and who it affects.

- Packing: a 200 GB memory task on a machine already half full (such tasks run on the high-memory pool, the 1 TB nodes from Chapter 2, because a standard 512 GB node can hold only a couple of them with anything else alongside).
- Locality: preferring machines that already have the data cached (back-reference Chapter 7).
- Licenses: a task that cannot start because a tool seat is not available (Chapter 2's licensing thread, and this chapter owns the scheduling side of it).
- The cost of a bad match: a task that waits while a machine idles.

> **Visual:** a lifecycle diagram of one task with the decision points marked, and who "owns" each.

### Fairness, in four flavors (≈750 words)

The core of the chapter. Each policy gets a one-paragraph story, a "what it rewards", and a "how it fails".

1. **Strict priority.** Simple, and the highest-priority show starves everyone else.
2. **First come, first served.** Feels fair; one huge submission blocks the afternoon.
3. **Weighted shares.** Each show gets a slice. Idle slices sit empty unless you allow borrowing.
4. **Fair share with decay and borrowing.** What we ended up with, and what we had to explain to people afterward.

Include a comparison table: policy, what it rewards, how it fails, who complains.

Include a short passage on **preemption**: the first time a long simulation was killed at 95% to let a short urgent render run, and how that changed the conversation. Foreshadow Chapter 20's checkpointing as the cure (the 40-hour simulation that dies at hour 39 is Chapter 12's and Chapter 20's story; here it is only the first cut).

### The scheduler as a bottleneck (≈450 words)

The "scheduler's own database" problem. How tracking millions of small tasks became its own scale problem: write rates, status chatter, a dashboard that took the system down by asking it too many questions.

- Reframe: batching tasks into **chunks** (several frames per task) trades scheduling overhead for coarser failure and retry (a thread that Chapter 20 picks up).
- A small cost-of-overhead example: a ten-second task that spends four seconds being scheduled.

> **Illustrative detail:** the first symptom was a status page that loaded slowly, then not at all. It took nearly two days before anyone connected it to the scheduler's own database, which was spending its mornings answering a dashboard. That database ceiling is also what eventually made replacing the scheduler unavoidable (Chapter 28).

### The politics of the queue (≈650 words)

The human half.

- The "bump my priority" inflation story and what finally stopped it.
- The tension between a fair queue and a *good* queue: the best use of the farm and the most equitable use are sometimes different.
- The delivery crunch: temporary boosts, who signs off, and how they expire (so they don't become permanent).
- Making the queue legible: a page that answers "why is my job waiting?" in plain words. Support requests dropped by about a third (illustrative), and arguments got better rather than fewer.
- A role for **someone who owns the queue**: part operator, part diplomat. Keep the role generic here; no pointer to another chapter is needed.

Keep the target of any joke the author's own early policy.

### The honest footnote (≈250 words)

What is still not solved: fairness across *kinds* of work (a ten-minute task versus a three-day simulation), the hidden cost of pre-emption, and the fact that humans optimize for the metric you publish. Mention that Chapter 22 will make usage visible in cost terms, which changes behavior again.

### Closing (≈200 words)

- Return to the 9:40 message and show how the answer sounds now: a link to the queue page and a two-line explanation.
- "What stuck": *the scheduler doesn't create scarcity; it just makes it visible, and people don't love being shown.*
- **Bridge to Chapter 20:** we can now start a million tasks in a night. What happens to the ones that don't finish?

## 6. Details to get right

- Stay product-neutral; describe concepts, not named schedulers. Say "a queue-based scheduler", never a product style.
- US spelling throughout (licenses, anonymized, behavior).
- Keep numbers plausible: a "million tasks a night" is the book's anchor figure (matches Ch. 20); do not inflate it. Jobs are about 240 tasks each, so about 4,000 jobs a night. Per-task memory (200 GB) is always said to be on the high-memory pool.
- Do not reveal show names, client names or real internal share allocations.
- Treat artists' queue requests respectfully; they are almost always legitimate.

## 8. Decisions taken (illustrative)

- Most contested policy: borrowing of idle shares. The show that lent the most was unhappiest; the fix was a decay rule everyone could read.
- Incident told here: the dashboard that took down its scheduler.
- The queue dashboard is open to the whole studio, and it changed behavior: fewer pleas, better arguments.
- "Ours" is fair share with decay and borrowing; strict priority and first-come-first-served are the cautionary tales.
- Scheduler-scale discussion here stays to the symptom and the lesson; the replacement itself belongs to Chapter 28.
