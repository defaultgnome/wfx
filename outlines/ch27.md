## 1. Purpose

Part VII closes with the question every pipeline team faces eventually: a brilliant paper lands on someone's desk and they say, "We should use this." Chapter 27 is about what happens in the six months after that sentence.

The chapter's thesis: *A good idea and a good fit are different things. The work of bringing research into production is mostly the work of finding out, early and cheaply, which one you have.*

If the reader remembers one sentence: *"A paper proves it can work. Production asks whether it will work on Tuesday, for everyone, on the worst shot."*

---

## 2. What the reader should walk away knowing

**Why research struggles in production**

- Papers show the best case on carefully chosen examples. Production lives in the worst case (the long tail from Ch. 2): the scene with ten times the geometry, the artist who uses the tool in a way nobody imagined.
- **What papers leave out:** robustness, memory, speed on real data, controls an artist can art-direct, and how it behaves when it fails.
- **Artist-directability** as a hard requirement: a method that is correct but can't be nudged is a method artists will turn off (echoing Ch. 10 and Ch. 14).
- The cost of integration: format support, versioning, the farm, previews that must agree with finals (Ch. 11), and support for everything downstream.

**A way of working**

- **Stages with exits**: reading, a small prototype, a test on real production data, a trial on a live shot, and wider rollout. Each stage has a question and a cheap way to say no.
- **Choosing the first problem** carefully: a pain artists already feel, not a solution looking for one.
- **The shot test:** run the idea on the three worst shots from the last show. Surprising how many beautiful methods end here.
- **Embedding with production:** having an artist in the loop from the first prototype, not at the demo.
- **Ownership at handover:** who maintains it after the researcher moves on? If no one volunteers, that's an answer.
- **Measuring success** in artist time and shot outcomes, not in benchmark numbers alone.

**How to kill a good idea**

- Why this is a skill. Ideas that fail on fit, not merit, deserve a respectful ending.
- Criteria for stopping: no artist pull, integration cost above value, a commercial or open-source alternative arriving, or the idea needing a rewrite of something too central.
- How to say it: write a short **closing note** (what we tried, what we learned, what would make us revisit it), thank the people involved, keep the prototype and the data.
- Distinguish "no" from "not yet": keep a shelf.

**Build versus adopt, again**

- Revisit the Chapter 3 criteria for research, with the callback to difference #4: each show re-tests every decision, and each new show's look may need a different answer: wait for the open-source implementation, license a commercial one, or write your own.
- Standing on others' work: open-source implementations of papers, community benchmarks, and the courtesy of contributing back where possible. Keep it generic.

**Skills for the reader to take away**

- A **research-to-production ladder** with exit questions at each rung.
- A **closing note template** for ideas that don't make it.
- A habit of asking "what is the worst shot for this?" before the first demo.

**Deliberately out of scope**

- Specific papers, algorithms or products, beyond one or two public, generic examples.
- Academic partnership contracts, patents, and publishing policy.
- Machine learning in the pipeline as a topic (Ch. 31); the ladder is where it gets tested first.

## 3. Narrative flow

### Opening hook (≈300 words)

Open with **a demo.** A prototype that makes everyone in the room gasp: a beautiful result on a hand-picked example, shown on a big screen. Then, a week later, the same method meets a real shot and quietly takes four hours, 300 gigabytes of memory (on the 1 TB high-memory pool; the standard 512 GB nodes would not have fit it), and produces something an artist describes as "interesting".

> **Illustrative contrast (chosen):** a learned denoising-style method dazzles on one hand-picked giant-forest frame, then takes four hours and 300 GB on the worst shot of the last show, and drifts in color on the mist. It dies at the prototype rung. The survivor is dull: a faster way to read big caches, tried on the three worst shots, handed over to a named owner, adopted in a quarter. The dull one earned more artist time than the dazzler ever would.

### The comfortable belief (≈150 words)

"If it works in the paper, it will work in the pipeline." Light humor about the effort of porting a one-figure result into a facility with a thousand people and nine years of history, and a new show every few months that makes it re-ask the build, buy or bolt-on question (difference #4, Ch. 3).

### The collision: the worst shot (≈600 words)

Walk through what happens when a promising method meets production:

- Data that doesn't look like the benchmark.
- A scale the authors never tried (Ch. 2, Ch. 9).
- Controls that can't be art-directed.
- A downstream system that assumed the old behavior.
- A support question at 2 a.m. (Ch. 25) that no one but the researcher can answer, and the researcher is on holiday.

Own the author's earlier enthusiasm plainly.

### The reframe: cheap, early, honest exits (≈600 words)

The shift in thinking. The goal of the first prototype is not to prove the idea works; it's to **find the way it breaks**. Reward people for killing ideas early. Design the stages so the cheapest tests come first and the artist sees it before the audience does.

> **Visual:** a funnel or ladder: many ideas, few reach production, each rung labeled with its exit question and rough cost.

### What we do now (≈750 words)

Practical habits, each with a trade-off.

- A research-to-production ladder, and who signs off at each rung.
- **The worst-shots benchmark:** a standing set of hard cases used to try anything new.
- **Pairing researchers with TDs and artists** from day one (back-reference Ch. 26).
- A handover checklist: docs, tests, an owner, a deprecation plan.
- **A shelf:** ideas stopped for now, with the conditions for revisiting. Occasionally the world catches up, and the shelf pays off.
- Using the build/adopt/bolt-on lens from Ch. 3 for each candidate.

State the costs: slower enthusiasm, more process, and the researcher who feels the ladder is bureaucracy.

### How to say no kindly (≈350 words)

A short, humane section. How to deliver the end of a project without ending a person's motivation. Specific language that gave credit to the work and explained the decision. A dry joke at the author's expense about how long a "quick prototype" was allowed to live.

> **Illustrative scene (chosen):** I stopped a promising simulation-acceleration prototype after fourteen weeks. I told the team in person, gave them the closing note first and my reasons second, and credited the work in the next all-hands. It landed badly for a day and fine for a week. One researcher kept the prototype on the shelf and, a year later, was right to.

### The honest footnote (≈250 words)

What still isn't solved. We killed one idea that later turned out to be right, and we shipped one that should have stayed on the shelf. Both are mine to own (illustrative). The shelf grows. Some research culture is lost as people move on.

### Closing (≈250 words)

- Return to the demo and show how the same meeting would go now, with the worst shot on the screen too.
- "What I'd tell my past self": *the best research outcome is sometimes a well-written no.*
- **Part wrap:** Part VII covered the people, tools and processes around the pipeline: plugins, crunch, the bridge role, and research.
- **Bridge to Part VIII:** we have covered the people, the tools and the processes that surround the pipeline. Part VIII turns to the hardest lessons, beginning with five migrations that nearly went wrong in Chapter 28.

## 5. Humor and tone opportunities

- The demo that gets applause and the shot that gets a long silence.
- The "quick prototype" that has been running for three years.
- The figure in the paper captioned "representative result".
- Researcher on holiday; production on fire.

Keep the target the author's own optimism. Never mock researchers or academics.

## 6. Details to get right

- Respect for research. The tone is "fit is hard", never "papers are naive".
- Keep any public references generic and accurate; avoid name-dropping papers or products.
- No confidential projects. Use composites.
- Be fair to artists' skepticism: it is often the best test the method will get.
